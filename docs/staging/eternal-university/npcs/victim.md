## The keystone (what makes him a person, not a plot node)

**He noticed everyone.** That's the whole man. Not "a brilliant
statistician" — a kid who genuinely *saw* people, especially the ones
nobody else bothered with: the night janitor, the boy on the floor who
never spoke, the woman who sweeps University Avenue. He knew their names and
used them. The demography is *downstream of the decency* — he didn't
discover the erased through math; he noticed they'd gone quiet because he
was the kind of person who'd notice, and the numbers only confirmed what his
attention already felt.

That's also the tragedy engine: **the flag-the-unreal method is the dark
inversion of his gift.** A boy who recognized people built a tool to detect
non-people — and the moment his warmth got systematized into a procedure, it
became the most dangerous thing in the world. He'd have hated what his
method could do. He is the player's moral predecessor: the win-loop is
*being known and knowing others*; Dunny **was** recognition, and it got him
killed.

## The contradiction (the Encyclopedia Brown method, and its resolution)

Different people knew different Dunnys, and the player's first real work is
reconciling them — so *we* hold the truth:

- **To the people he looked out for** (the night janitor, the quiet kids he
  greeted): a kind friend who actually listened.
- **To the gossip layer:** shady, a *dealer*, always angling.
- **To the proctors:** an odd kid, coming and going at all hours, a bit of a
  problem.
- **To his demography professor:** obsessive, gifted, a little unwell about
  it.

None are lies. They resolve into one person: a kind, obsessive kid running a
**secret investigation** that made him furtive. The "dealing" was him
bartering favors for access to records and quietly looking out for people;
the odd hours were registrar work and chasing a discrepancy that wouldn't
close. The contradictions *are* the negative space — you assemble the real
him from the gaps between how he was seen.

## The flaw / the kill (trait, not power)

He couldn't let an error round away. Hand him a count that's off by three and
he'd lose a week to it — Pi's Max, the cautionary version of the player. The
gift **isolated** him: the last weeks he was sleeping in the library, missing
his actual classes, running on the thread, and *scared, without saying so.*
And he died the way the story needs him to — **alone**, because he tried to
verify it himself. He had the macro signal and no circle; one person, one
room, easy to silence. Everything the player will do right, he did alone.

## Concrete texture (the clean lines — few, sharp)

- **The laugh through the wall** — you knew him by a sound, not a face. The
  floor is quieter now and can't say why.
- **He counted under his breath** — stairs, windows, the people in a room. A
  demographer's tic; charming and a little odd. When anxious, the counting
  sped up. People remember the muttered numbers.
- **The running list everyone assumed was schoolwork.** It was the people he
  was worried about — the ones going quiet. His "sample" was his friends.
- **Always mid-favor** — owed and owing, trading small things. Looked like
  dealing. Was loyalty.
- **The cold tea** — the unfinished cup gone cold on the desk. A small
  cooling thing; algor mortis of a life. The negative-space object.

## The body (his autopsy — the player's first forensic win)

Official story: an **accident** — fell down Duncan Hall's service stairs
(the dorm's verticality), found at the bottom, case closed, proctors
relieved. The forensic contradiction, model-grounded (§14): **algor mortis
says he died hours before the filed time, and the body's too cold for a warm
stairwell** — it cooled somewhere cold first and was *moved*. The cooling
history doesn't fit one place. He was killed elsewhere and staged. (Optional
secondary: a faint sedative in the digestion buffer — moved without a
struggle. Keep the **time-and-temperature mismatch** as the primary, clean
clue; don't lean on wound pathology, which the model doesn't really support
yet.) The scene and the body audit each other, as §14 promises — and it's
the moment the "accident" dies.

## The room (the sealed setpiece — negative space doing the work)

Sealed under proctor's tape; they cleared it as routine and never grasped
what it held. A *lived-in* room cut off mid-life — the cold tea, the unmade
bed, the coursework he'd stopped keeping up. **It's also half-furnished:**
the victim's side intact (preserved as evidence), the *roommate's* side
stripped and hastily boxed (the roommate was moved "to preserve evidence" —
see the roommate carve). Two kinds of absence in one room: a life stopped vs.
a life evacuated.

The work is **hidden in plain sight**, disguised in his counting-notation
(reads as doodles and number-columns; *is* the cross-reference of the rolls
against who's actually recognized). The notebooks. And the gut-punch buried
in it: **the list of names turns out to be people he loved** — the
uncountable, the ones he watched, his real study and his real friends. His
"data" had names he grieved. The method itself is a **procedure, not a
machine** (§8 — the aether does cognition, not gadgets): a way to flag the
unreal, sitting there waiting for someone less kind to pick it up.

> **Note:** one fragment of the work is *not* in this room — Dunny tucked it
> into the roommate's belongings, and it rode out in their boxes when they
> were moved. The sealed room is deliberately *incomplete*. See the roommate
> carve.

## The hole he left (the negative-space summary)

The floor's quieter. The people he greeted go unacknowledged again — and a
couple of them are frightened, or already gone. A new arrival — you — gets the
room down the hall, feels the silence is *wrong*, and can't stop poking at it
(the organic on-ramp; no quest-giver — see the bible's §12). The proctors want
the room sealed and the story soft. That absence, campus-shaped, is the carve.
