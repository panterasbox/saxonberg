# #79 · The Lens of Freedom

> One of the [Schell deck](./README.md). Lens named from *A Book of
> Lenses*; questions paraphrased, analysis our own.[^aogd-fd]
>
> ⚠ **Opened as a short entry sharing one answer with
> [#31 Action](./31-action.md). Expanded 2026-09-29**, because the real
> answer is not a mechanism — it is that **freedom here is a political
> question wearing a design question's clothes.**

## The lens

A feeling of freedom is one of the things separating games from other
entertainment. **When do players have freedom of action, and do they
feel free then? When are they constrained, and do they feel
constrained? Where could they feel freer? And are there places where
they are overwhelmed by too much freedom?**

> **From the book.** He frames it as the resolution of an older
> tension: *"we touched on the conflict between story and gameplay. At
> its heart, **this is a conflict about freedom**."* The chapter opens
> with John Lennon at the Indica Gallery in 1966 — a stepladder, a
> blank canvas on the ceiling, a magnifying glass on a chain. He climbs
> the wobbly ladder, cranes his neck, and finds one tiny word: **"yes."**

## Which of our seven it sharpens

**[Lens 2 · Creative expression](../design-lenses.md)** and
**[lens 3a · Immersion](../design-lenses.md)** — the sandbox promise
and the honest boundary, respectively.

## ⭐⭐⭐ What the design answers — two regimes, and one flag

Schell assumes the designer decides what a player may do. **Here the
designer mostly decides what may be *decided*.**

### The shared world — freedom is granted by governance

Call security, parcel extents and committees answering to the Compact
are what actually bound a player, and **almost none of that is the
platform's opinion.** Permissions ride grouping — several group
mechanisms behind one facade — and who is in which group is staffed by
**offices and committees the polity fills as it sees fit.**

> ⭐⭐⭐ **There is exactly one platform-level class of player: the
> wizard flag**, which gates authoring JavaScript. Everything else is
> governance.

That is an unusual answer to *when are they constrained*, and it is not
available to a single-player game: **most constraints here are
amendable by somebody the player can argue with.** The freedom on offer
is not only *you may do this* but **you may argue that you should be
allowed to** — which is a kind of liberty his lens has no category for,
because a game with one designer cannot offer it.

⚠ **The platform is opinionated and should say so.** It is architected
to afford a particular level of player freedom; it locks the shared
world down deliberately, to keep people with perverse incentives from
acting on their worst impulses. *"We give you the controls; we impose
nothing"* is the answer every attention company gives
([measurement.md](../measurement.md)) and it is not ours.

### Your own sandbox — freedom is maximal

The flip side, and the part that makes the lockdown affordable:
**every player gets a circle in which they can create anything they can
imagine.** The boundary is symmetric by invariant — nothing crosses in
or out — which is precisely what lets the inside be unconstrained.

### And the ladder between them binds differently at each rung

> **Content can be made code and code can be made content, and the two
> bind in different ways.**

| rung | what you may do | who may widen it |
|---|---|---|
| **play** | act within the world | the polity, by office and committee |
| **content** | author rows, templates, documents, packs | the polity, and parcel title |
| **code** | write executable JavaScript | ⛔ **nobody but the wizard axis** — *content-write never grants code execution* (A9) |

⭐ The middle rung is where governance actually operates, and it is much
wider than most games' *modding*. The top rung is the one the polity
cannot staff, which is [#37](./37-fairness.md)'s wizard problem seen
from the other side: **the same fact reads as an unfair asymmetry there
and as the one ungoverned freedom here.**

### The door is a mechanism, not the answer

Where a boundary *is* met, it names itself: since verb conferral was
retired, **a locked door says what it is and what would open it.** That
converts his *feeling* question into a structural one for the
constrained half — and it is why a refusal naming no remedy fails two
lenses at once, freedom here and solvability at
[#55](./55-visible-progress.md).

⭐ **And the Lennon anecdote is a better fit than it first looks.** The
reward for an awkward, constrained, effortful act was one word, and the
word was *yes*. That is the shape of a refusal lifted — **the
constraint is what made the payoff mean anything**, which is the
argument for keeping boundaries legible rather than sanding them off.

## The verdict

⭐⭐⭐ **Push back on the frame: he is asking a design question that is
mostly ours to delegate.** Adopt his fourth. Concede his first three
have no instrument.

### The reframe

*When do players have freedom of action?* presumes a designer holding a
dial. Our dial has three settings and only one of them is ours: **the
sandbox (maximal, by construction), the shared world (whatever the
polity grants), and code (one flag).** A lens pass on a new feature
should therefore not ask *how free does this make the player* but
**which regime does this constrain, and who can lift it?**

### The question we have not asked

> *Are there any places where they are **overwhelmed by too much
> freedom**?*

**Lens 2 manufactures this problem on purpose.** *Build whatever you
want and show it off* is a blank canvas, and a blank canvas is
paralysing to most people most of the time. Nothing in the rubric asks
whether an expressive surface is *enterable* — only whether it is
expressive. A sandbox nobody knows how to start using scores full marks
on lens 2 and produces nothing.

⚠ This is the same finding [#55](./55-visible-progress.md) reaches from
the other side — *a pull nobody knows to pull is not an answer* — which
suggests it is one gap, not two: **the design is consistently better at
making things possible than at making them startable.**

### The three we cannot answer

*Do they feel free? Do they feel constrained? Could they feel freer?*
are all about **feeling**, and every answer we have is about
**structure**. We can demonstrate a door is honest; we cannot
demonstrate it feels like liberty rather than a corridor.

That is the same substitution [#25 Judgment](./25-judgment.md) records —
we trade an uncheckable feeling for a reviewable criterion, correctly —
and it carries the same cost: **nothing in our process ever asks how the
boundary lands.** It cannot be a design-review question. It is a
playtest question or it is nothing.

## Tensions & risks

⚠⚠ **The two regimes have opposite failure modes, so "more freedom" is
never globally right.** The shared world's risk is **over-constraint** —
bureaucracy, refusal density, a committee for everything. The sandbox's
risk is **under-structure** — the blank canvas above. A prescription
that improves one worsens the other, and the rubric currently has one
word for both.

⚠⚠ **A sandbox that cannot export is freedom without consequence.** The
symmetric boundary is what makes maximal interior freedom safe, and it
is also what makes it **sterile**: anything made in there matters to
nobody until it crosses. Lens 2's published flag is the crossing, and
until it exists the sandbox's liberty is real and inconsequential —
which is a different failure from being constrained, and arguably worse.

⚠⚠ **An honest door is not automatically a welcome one.** *"You cannot
do this yet, and here is what would let you"* is truthful and can still
read as a bureaucracy — a world of accurate refusals is a world that
says no a great deal. The remedy-naming makes each refusal fair; it does
nothing about their **density**.

⚠ **Story and freedom are in tension here, and our quests are where it
bites.** He is explicit that the story/gameplay conflict *is* a freedom
conflict. Our position — the world generates stories, quests are the
declared authored exception — means every quest is a local reduction in
freedom, traded for meaning. That trade is currently unexamined.

## Implications

1. ⭐⭐ **Add enterability to lens 2.** An expressive surface that is not
   startable has not landed. One line in the test, and it catches the
   blank-canvas failure that *expressiveness* alone cannot see.
2. ⭐ **Count refusals, not just audit them.** Each may be fair while
   the aggregate reads as a locked building. Density is a reviewable
   property; fairness per-refusal already is.
3. ⭐⭐ **State the two regimes in the rubric.** A feature's freedom
   question is *which regime, and who can lift it* — not *how free*.
   That is a one-line change that makes most of this lens mechanical.
4. ⭐ **Keep the door doing double duty, and say so.** The same
   mechanism answers #31's discovery half, #55's solvability, and this
   lens's constrained half — which is an argument for treating refusal
   text as **first-class content** rather than error strings.
5. **Playtest the boundary, since review cannot.** *Did the player feel
   free* is unanswerable at a desk.
