## The lens

Think about the player's true motivations — not the goals your game
sets, but the reason the player wants them. Three questions: **what
questions does my game put into the player's mind? What am I doing to
make them care about those questions? What can I do to make them
invent even more questions?**

> **From the book.** The lens lands at the end of Schell's hunt for a
> definition of play, and the definition is the lens's real cargo:
> **"Play is manipulation that indulges curiosity"** — the artist
> "playing with color," the chemist "playing with an idea." His
> proof-case is Rico, the assembly-line worker who races his own
> record: the moment the reason for the activity becomes a *personal
> question* ("can I beat my record?"), work becomes play — same
> actions, different reason. The lens's worked example is small and
> exact: a timed maze poses "can I solve it in 30 seconds?", and a
> reward animation layers a second question ("what's the next one?") —
> curiosity compounds when each answer plants the next
> question.[^aogd-cu]

## Why our design prompts it

Rico is the education vertical's whole theory of value, stated by
Schell in one anecdote. Coursework is obligated work with an urgent
goal — exactly what the [high-stakes
reconciliation](../study-com-strategy.md) says we must not compete
with. Rico's line names what the game actually *does* to it: **the
world manufactures personal questions about the subject.** "Can I
finally wire the substation?" is a personal question whose answer
happens to be the curriculum. Same coursework, different reason —
obligation converted to curiosity, which is the only conversion that
turns study into play without touching the instrument.

## What the design answers

### Two curiosity economies

The original entry knew only the first:

- **Authored curiosity** — mysteries written into content: Dr. Limen,
  Gus's watch, the tunnels, "who made this place." Finite, potent,
  and *debt-bearing*: every planted question owes a designed answer
  or a deliberately framed open mystery, and teasing without payoff
  teaches players to stop wondering. (Still true; still the
  discipline.)
- **Systemic curiosity** — questions no one authored, generated by
  honest composition: *why didn't the wet wood light? Does the
  firebreak trick work through a window? If metal worsens shock, does
  rubber armor save me in the brine pool?* This economy is
  **infinite and self-paying** — the payoff is the model's own honest
  behavior, delivered automatically, no content debt incurred. It is
  the comparative advantage of modeling causes: a fudged game's "why"
  bottoms out at *because the designer said so*; ours bottoms out at
  reality — and Schell's third question ("make them invent even more
  questions") is answered by the substrate itself, because every
  honest answer exposes the next variable to wiggle.

### Curiosity got mechanized since the original entry

- **Concealment/detection** made "what's here?" a real question with
  real answers: concealment bands, honest fog at every enumeration
  seam, the costed `search`, passive hints, hidden exits with reveal
  paths.
- **Discovery is remembered.** The DISCOVERY belief realm is
  permanent per-viewer found-memory — the game literally keeps a
  ledger of your answered curiosities. Finding a thing is a fact
  about *you* now.
- **Attention is an economy.** `awareness` is a Discipline;
  care-vs-speed pace modes trade attention against time; searching
  costs something. Curiosity isn't a free camera — it's a practiced,
  improvable capacity, which makes it *advancement* (curiosity as a
  skill you get better at is itself a question generator).
- **The subject bridge is built now.** The old entry's top implication
  — "build the curiosity→subject bridge on purpose" — is no longer an
  aspiration: capability ceilings are the bridge. The wall you hit is
  honest (you genuinely can't do the thing safely), the question it
  plants is personal (Rico's), and the answer is the discipline —
  in-game study or, at the vertical's ceiling, real coursework
  through the credential seam. `analyze` is the standing invitation
  to ask *why* about anything; the ZPD gate makes "what could I learn
  next?" a first-class question.

### Platform coda: the fifth clause is curiosity's warranty

"The world itself honors what you understand" (the ratified fifth
essence clause) is, from this lens's angle, a *warranty on
curiosity*: no honest question is ever wasted here, because every
why has a real answer and every answer is operative. That warranty
is what lets world-curiosity convert to subject-curiosity without a
bait-and-switch — the world question and the subject question have
the same answer.

## Tensions & risks

- **Authored curiosity still carries the debt.** The big hooks
  (Limen's nature, Gus's secret, the campus's origin) owe designed
  answers or framed-open status. Systemic curiosity pays for itself;
  lore does not. Audit stands.
- **Text plants questions by authoring, not by horizon.** A shape in
  the distance is free in graphics; text writes every lure. The
  finish technique (strange surface, familiar function) remains the
  cheap text-native planter — and unauthored rooms still provoke
  nothing. Systemic curiosity softens this (a fire in *any* room is
  interesting) but doesn't erase it.
- **Systemic curiosity needs the toy's handles.** A question the
  player can't investigate (no `analyze`, no legibility surface, no
  instrument) dies unanswered even though the answer exists. The
  Toy's rule — every channel ships with its handles or it's plumbing
  — is also this lens's rule.
- **Personal questions can't be assigned — but they can be
  invited.** Rico's question worked because it was *his*. A quest
  is a legitimate invitation (an authored arc that walks you up to
  the wall and makes you care about what's behind it); what it
  can't do is *substitute* for the personal question — the player
  who finishes the arc still has to want the answer for
  themselves. Design quests as question-planters, and keep the
  capability wall doing its unassigned work underneath.

## Implications

1. **Rico goes in the pitch vocabulary.** "The game manufactures
   personal questions about the subject" is the lens-grounded,
   one-sentence answer to *what does the game do to learning* — and
   it's assert-don't-defend compatible: nothing about the coursework
   changes; the reason does. (Feeds
   [study-com-strategy.md](../study-com-strategy.md) and whichever
   video carries the conferral experience.)
2. **Bank on systemic curiosity; budget authored curiosity.** New
   content should default to planting questions the *model* can
   answer (a strange material, a sealed room, a wet floor) before
   questions only a lore doc can answer. Authored mysteries are a
   luxury good with a payoff debt; spend deliberately.
3. **The curiosity audit joins the slate checklist:** *what question
   does this system put in the player's mind, and can the player
   investigate it with shipped handles?* (Pairs with the Toy's
   handles rule and Infinite Inspiration's name-your-inspiration.)
4. **Quests invite the question; the wall makes it personal.** The
   capability ceiling stays the fundamental question-planter, and
   authored arcs are the narrative on-ramps that bring players to
   walls they'd never have found alone. The one discipline: a
   quest may make you *care* about the substation; it must never
   reduce the learning itself to a checklist item — the arc ends
   at the wall, and crossing it is yours.
5. **Layer secrets so depth survives the first answer** — Gus's
   watch remains the authored model: a freely-shown surface with
   lore beneath. Unchanged from the original entry.
