# Lens: The Avatar

> Part of the [design lenses](./README.md) set. Lens named from Jesse
> Schell's *A Book of Lenses*; questions paraphrased, analysis our own.
>
> **Layer interrogated: the game** (char-gen + embodiment are the
> player's in-world self).

## The lens

The avatar is the player's foothold in the world — the self they project
into it. A strong avatar is a vehicle for that projection: sometimes an
idealized version of the player, sometimes a blank-enough vessel to
inhabit, but always a bridge between the person and the place. The
questions: how does the avatar let the player project themselves? Is it
an ideal they aspire to, a blank they fill in, or a character they
wear — and does that match the experience I want?

> **From the book.** Schell's avatar is "a player's gateway into the
> world of the game," and his two questions are pointed: "Is my avatar an
> *ideal form* likely to resonate with my players?" and "Does my avatar
> have *iconic qualities* that let a player project themselves into the
> character?" — the compelling ideal and the abstracted blank, the two
> ways a face becomes "ours." His blunt premise: "people don't play games
> to be themselves — they play games to be the people they wish they
> could be." The avatar isn't a puppet; it's the player's projected,
> idealized self.[^aogd-av]

## Why our design prompts it

Because character generation *is* avatar design, and this game makes
deliberate, unusual choices about it: "light by design," "depth is
earned, not chosen," a student rather than a hero, and a dorm room that
becomes an extension of the self. Each of those is an answer to "how does
the player project?" — and one of them runs against Schell's idealized-
avatar grain.

## What the design answers

- **Identity, not a stat sheet.** Char-gen is name, species, pronouns,
  and a backstory/aspiration — "light by design," with depth earned in
  play, not allocated at creation
  ([char-gen](../slates/char-gen-slate.md)). The avatar is a *person*,
  not a build.
- **A projective aspiration.** You pick who you want to *become*
  (Something Better / Healer / Teacher / Guardian / Founder / Seeker) —
  projection aimed at a future self, which is a sharp fit for a game
  about growth.
- **A relatable vessel.** The avatar is a *student* — not a chosen-one,
  a newcomer like the player. Low idealization, high relatability.
- **The dorm as avatar-extension.** The authored room is the self made
  visible — the onboarding climax is, in avatar terms, the first place
  the player's identity becomes concrete in the world.
- **A real body, seen by others.** The embodiment substrate (body plans,
  wearables, appearance presets) gives the avatar physical presence;
  recognition means others perceive its identity (name on sight, role in
  shortDescription).

## Tensions & risks

- **"Become, don't begin-as" runs against the idealized avatar.** Schell
  is blunt that "people don't play games to be themselves — they play
  games to be the people they wish they could be," and asks whether the
  avatar is "an ideal form"; this one is a humble freshman who *grows*.
  That's a deliberate, pedagogically-aligned
  inversion (the learning frame *is* growth-of-self) — but idealization
  is part of what drives avatar attachment, so the design trades some
  early projective pull for a growth arc. The bet is that becoming beats
  being; it should be made knowingly.
- **Light char-gen delays self-expression.** Closed-choice plus heavy
  defaults means the avatar is under-customized at birth (deferred to
  in-world services). Players who want to express identity *up front*
  are constrained; the design bets they'll express it through play and
  the dorm instead. That bet only pays if the deferred expression
  surfaces are rich.
- **Two identity axes that don't obviously cohere.** Fantasy species
  (Elf, Dwarf, Tiefling) sit beside an academic aspiration on an
  un-genred campus. "Familiar canon players main" justifies the roster,
  but a fantasy body in an un-genred university is a slight friction —
  which the dorm's "genre-mashup at personal scale" suggests the design
  actually *embraces* rather than resolves.

## Implications

1. **Name and own the "become, don't begin-as" avatar model.** It's a
   deliberate inversion of the idealized avatar and it's *right* for a
   learning game — but make it conscious, and make sure the early
   attachment the humble start gives up is repaid (the dorm-as-self is
   the payoff; lean on it).
2. **Make the deferred expression surfaces rich enough to justify the
   delay.** Char-gen defers self-expression to in-world services and the
   dorm; the dorm delivers, but appearance/outfitter and the registrar
   path must also feel like real self-authoring, or the light intake
   reads as thin rather than welcoming.
3. **Decide whether the two identity axes mash or reconcile.** Fantasy
   species + academic aspiration on an un-genred campus is either
   charming genre-mashup or incoherence; the dorm suggests *embrace it*
   — make that a conscious stance rather than an unexamined inheritance.
