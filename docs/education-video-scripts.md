### Narration (draft)

**[1 — cold open]** *(screen: the cockpit — prose center, panels
lit, no logos, no music swell; the cursor already typing)*
This is a fountain in a text-based role-playing game.

*(screen: `look fountain` → the authored prose renders — granite
basin, bronze spout, moving water; prose TBD with the fountain
build)*
Nice prose. Every game has prose. Now watch this.

**[2 — drill 1: it has chemistry]** *(screen: `analyze chemistry
the spout`, output landing line by line — let the render breathe)*
`analyze`. A command any player can type, on anything in the
world. The spout comes back: bronze. Density, 8,800 kilograms per
cubic meter. Composition — 88 percent copper.

Keep going. Analyze the copper.

Symbol, Cu. Atomic number, 29. Molar mass, 63.546.

**[3 — drill 2: it's the real chemistry (the moment)]** *(screen:
the game's copper readout beside a periodic-table tile —
generated asset, see cue sheet)*
That number should look familiar. Pause the video — pull up a
periodic table. Any one. Copper: 63.546. This isn't a stat sheet
doing a chemistry impression. It's the same table your textbook
prints. If you ever memorized that number for a quiz — here it
is, holding up a world.

**[4 — the rest beat: the rule underneath (beat #9)]** *(screen:
the two looks side by side — the prose and the readout)*
One rule makes that work: the prose and the numbers are two
renderings of one model. The description you read at the start
isn't flavor text draped over data — it *is* the data, in
language. When the model can't support a sentence, the sentence
doesn't get written.

**[5 — drill 3: point it at anything]** *(montage, three
captures: a species dossier; a meal's nutrient readout; `measure
pressure` at the bottom of a staircase, then the top — the derived
altitude)*
Now point it at anything. Every species carries a real taxonomy;
every meal, real nutrition. And the world doesn't just store the
numbers — it computes with them: take a barometer up a staircase,
and the game derives your altitude from the pressure drop. That's
the barometric formula, running under your feet. It goes down as
far as your curiosity does.

**[6 — the turn]** *(screen: cut from the game to the CMS — the
world's source tree; the material shelf scrolls past; `copper.yaml`
opens; the number highlighted)*
Now flip it around. Everything you've just read lives in a file.
This is the CMS — the world's source, browsable: a shelf of
materials, the species, the spells. Open copper — and there's your
63.546, right where the world keeps it. So sit in the creator's
chair, and add something to the shelf.

**[7 — drill 4: state facts, get a world]** *(screen: `silver.yaml`
typed live — the real numbers landing one by one; go-live; then
`analyze chemistry the silver ingot` in-world; `analyze weapon`
on a silver dagger; the readout-vs-reference card for the thermal
conductivity)*
Say you want silver in the world. You don't script behaviors. You
state facts: density, 10,490. Melting point, 1235 Kelvin. Thermal
conductivity, 429. Go live. The world knows silver now. Give a
dagger that material and it derives its own handling — reach,
guard, balance — from mass and form; nobody hand-tunes it.
Furnace-side, it melts at exactly the temperature you declared.
And run the check again — pull the handbook: silver carries heat
better than copper. Better than any metal there is. Most people
guess wrong on that. The world doesn't.

**[8 — drill 5: invent an artifact (the moment)]** *(screen: the
wand's YAML — the material line pointing at your silver; the
effect list, short enough to read whole)*
One more chair. Make something magical — a silver wand of frost.
The wand is a page of data too: the material — yours, from a
minute ago — and the effects it fires. And because effects ride
the world's real systems, one honest concept does many believable
things: this wand freezes a puddle solid, shatters a glass,
chills a fever down. That's also the limit. The effect list is a
closed vocabulary of things the world already does honestly: pull
heat out, and real thermodynamics decides what freezes; pour heat
in, and real combustion takes over. Write "gain five levels" —
and the world won't parse the sentence. You can invent magic. You
can't invent lies.

**[9 — finale: back to the fountain]** *(screen: the fountain
from minute one — same framing as the cold open, deliberately;
the wand fired into the basin; the prose of the freeze, slow
push-in)*
So take your wand back to where this video started. The water in
that basin has a file too, and the file has said all along:
freezes at 273 Kelvin — and exactly how much heat has to leave it
first. Fire into the basin. The wand pulls real heat out of real
water, the world computes the phase change, and the fountain goes
solid — at precisely the temperature its file promised. Your
material, your magic, the world's physics. And there's the whole
design in one object. Learn it in a classroom, and you already
know it here. Learn it here, and you know it everywhere. You
study because your character needs it — the knowledge is
load-bearing for the student who learns it, and for the creator
who writes it.

**[10 — the cost, the thesis, out]** *(screen: the cockpit wide —
stream center, panels around it; lower-third card for the thesis
line)*
The trade is real: text is serial — you read one line at a time;
that's what the panels are for. What it buys is a world with no
art budget between the model and the player. The ceiling is
imagination, not rendering.

Because underneath everything in this video there's one person: a
student who wants to learn real things and use them somewhere
that answers back. That's the engine — curiosity, then study,
then a world that visibly gives way to what you learned. Delight
as the reward for understanding.

So here's the read for anyone who builds education products —
anyone whose asset is subject-matter expertise: here, expertise is
*executable*. Facts are content. In an era where prose can be
generated in seconds, content can be commoditized; an honest world
can't be — it has to be modeled, checked, and kept honest, and
that work compounds. And a world made of words is already
denominated in the currency AI spends: the interface a person
reads and types is, by construction, one a machine can read and
emit. **[THEME — one sentence, unratified; candidates below]**
This model generalizes to any domain honest enough to measure —
and education is its first and best home.

### Narration (draft)

**[1 — cold open: the wall]** *(client full-bleed; the Hearthworks
foundry — the furnace lit, the bronze charged, a commissioned
casting waiting; the melt attempted; the world's refusal renders)*
This is a foundry in a text-based role-playing game — and this is
a pour my character can't make. Not because a quest is locked.
The furnace is lit, the bronze is charged, and she doesn't know
how much heat it takes to bring twenty kilos of metal from cold
to pouring — so if she guesses, the melt goes cold halfway, and
the casting is scrap. The world will let it happen. That's the
wall. And it's honest: the world computes the heat for real, and
she hasn't learned to.

**[2 — drill 1: the wall is honest]** *(the transcript / bands
surface; her smithing band, two rungs short)*
Here's why, on one screen. Every character carries a transcript —
a record of what they've done and what they've studied — and
competence is *derived* from that evidence. No points, no XP bar;
you're shown a band, and her smithing band is two rungs short of
the furnace. And this isn't a hobby: she works here — the shift
pays her rent, and a scrapped casting comes out of somebody's
ledger. Now notice what the wall just did. "Can I run this melt?"
is suddenly *my* question. It's the same material a syllabus
would assign — with a completely different reason to learn it.

**[3 — drill 2: study crosses the seam]** *(the course surface
in-world → real study.com capture, tonight's chapter → back to the
character sheet as the claim row lands)*
So we study. The course sits right there in the world, and it's a
real one — study.com, the same general-chemistry course a student
takes for college credit. And the chapter is the one everybody
remembers: thermochemistry — specific heat, latent heat, how much
energy it takes to turn solid metal liquid. Finish tonight's
chapter and
watch the transcript: the completion crosses over and lands as a
*claim* — the world now knows what she's studied, and the doors
that need knowledge start opening. One aside, once: everything
I've clicked in this video has been quietly typing commands —
every button here is a handle on one language. That's the whole
interface.

**[4 — drill 3: the final]** *(the identity-verified final; the
event crosses; the band moves on screen)*
Then the real test — the course's identity-verified final, the
one behind its credit recommendation. Pass it, and one event
crosses the seam carrying exactly how it was earned — verified,
credit-recommended — and lands as the heaviest evidence a
classroom can mint. The band crosses. And a band crossing here
doesn't award points. It confers *capability*.

**[5 — the conferral (the moment)]** *(the University; the
registrar's hall; small, diegetic, no fanfare)*
Which the world marks properly. Walk into the University and the
registrar already knows — reads the new line off your transcript,
says the words, and the verbs are hers: charge the furnace, run
the melt, pour. No fireworks, no jingle. A threshold, crossed,
witnessed. The world recognizes what you now are.

**[6 — the return (the proof)]** *(back to the foundry; the same
command from the cold open; it runs; the charge, the plateau, the
pour; the casting holds)*
Now go back. Same foundry, same fire, same command the world
refused in the first minute of this video — and it runs. She
charges the furnace for the mass she's melting, holds it through
the plateau where the heat keeps going in and the temperature
won't move — exactly like the chapter said it would, because
that's what latent heat is — and the pour fills the mold, because
the furnace runs on the same equation that was on her final. The
furnace doesn't care what she believes. It cares what she knows.
The wall didn't move. She did — and the knowledge came with her.

**[7 — descent: the exec read, out]** *(face returns; the cockpit
wide; lower-third for the read)*
That's the loop from the inside: a wall that plants a question,
study that answers it, and a world that recognizes the answer.
And for the people who build the learning platform, look at what
the integration was. The game consumed two events you already
record — a chapter completed, a final passed — and it never
touched your instrument. You stay the issuer; the world makes
your signal worth more. And completion — the metric everyone
chases — happened because a character was waiting on it.
**[THEME — one sentence, unratified; candidates below]**
Completion, felt from the inside. That's the integration.
