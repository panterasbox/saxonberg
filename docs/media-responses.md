answers, or the world it is a response to. Each entry gives the citation, the

## 1. IGN, *"How MMORPGs Built and Lost the Live-Service Throne"*

> **Source:** Sab Astley, IGN, 2026 — part one of IGN's three-part
> live-service series (part two: *"How live-service turned gaming into a
> bloody battle royale"*).
> <https://www.ign.com/articles/how-mmorpgs-built-and-lost-the-live-service-throne>
> Interviewees: **Starr Long** (director, *Ultima Online*), **Bill
> Trost** (co-creator, *EverQuest*), **Jeff Kaplan** (*World of
> Warcraft*, later *Overwatch*). Read and discussed 2026-10-10.

**The article's argument, briefly.** MMORPGs were the first live-service
games. Their center was a shared world that became a community — *"one
is a battlefield, the other is home."* Live service kept the MMO's
massive populations and dropped the world, moving to short "jump in,
jump out" cycles driven by the battle pass. The closing image: in the
2000s **games were the destination; in 2026 they have become the
airport**, a departure board of separate islands (Fortnite's islands,
Roblox's worlds-upon-worlds). It ends on Roblox's creator economy and
asks *"can these platforms keep up?"*

**The quotes worth keeping** (verbatim, attributed as in the article):

- Starr Long, on UO's vomit protest at Lord British's castle: *"That was
  the turning point where we realized, while we are the game creators,
  this is the players' world. We're just facilitating it for them."*
- Long: *"We realized that running one of these games is more like
  running a government. Your people are your citizens."*
- Bill Trost: *"My world-building philosophy was to build a world with
  integrity, but don't design it to death."*
- Trost: *"The ubiquitous nature of community doesn't feel confined to
  the game anymore — it's fragmented out across all our social media."*
- Trost: *"The table stakes required to even be considered by the gaming
  public are now so high it becomes difficult to justify the
  investment it takes."*
- Long: *"The market overall has moved to much, much shorter play cycles
  over the last two decades."*
- The article on UO's founding novelty: *"a world that could change
  whilst they weren't playing."*

**How each lands on Saxonberg:**

| The article says | Saxonberg |
|---|---|
| UO learned its players were a polity — by accident, from a protest | Starts there. Lens 3b measures participation by *can the polity do something we did not want* |
| Long: running an MMO is *like* running a government | Not a simile here — the Compact, the chambers, the record. **And the citizens are the developers** (below) |
| A world that changes while you're away was UO's novelty | Every system's default: reconcile-on-read growth, maturation, a pet's off-screen life, a fishery recovering on its half-life |
| Trost: integrity, but don't design it to death | Lens 1 (a derivable world) + *trade is mechanism, locality is expression* |
| Community fragmented onto social media | The market thesis — the competitor is Discord; give the community back a place that *remembers* ([positioning § 4d](./positioning.md)) |
| Shorter play cycles | One login = one tend decision; a 12× clock over reconcile-on-read means a short session matters in a persistent world |
| Destination → airport | **Both, without the trade-off** (below) |

**⭐⭐⭐ What the article doesn't reach — and what we say.** It never
says *why* the genre stalled beyond cost. Its own numbers do: WoW has
over 10,000 unique hand-written quests. **The MMO's content was a
treadmill the studio had to keep running**, and the battle pass is the
same treadmill made cheaper (*"eliminate four players"*).

Roblox, its proposed successor, solved the treadmill by making players
the developers — **and paid for it with the world.** Each creator gets
an island. That is the airport.

**Saxonberg: the players are the game developers, of one shared world,
regulated by the Compact.** The platform's builders make the platform;
everything a player meets — places, trades, businesses, stories, law —
comes from the players. Thousands of developers building in one world
need to be governed, and **that is what the Compact is for.** Long's
line turns literal: the citizens of this government *are* its studio.
And the article's closing question — *can these platforms keep up?* —
stops being a content-production question and becomes a governance one.

⚠ **Framing rule this produced:** never write "the developer authors the
content." Whoever builds the platform is the *platform* developer; the
*game* developers are the players.

**Copy seeds** (drafts, in [positioning](./positioning.md) Part 4's voice):

> Games used to be destinations. Now they are airports — a departure
> board of islands, each one somebody else's. We think a world can be
> built by its players and still be one world.

> The MMO's makers learned, by accident, that they were running a
> government. We started there — and made the citizens the studio.

> Your community doesn't need another island. It needs a place.

## 2. WIRED, *"World Models Will Soon Be Inescapable. Here's What You Need to Know"*

> **Source:** Reece Rogers, WIRED (Gear), 2026-11-08.
> <https://www.wired.com/story/world-models-will-soon-be-inescapable-what-you-need-to-know/>
> Interviewees: **Brian McClendon** (CTO, Niantic Spatial), **Oliver Cameron**
> (CEO, Odyssey), **Jamie Shotton** (chief scientist, Wayve). Read and discussed
> 2026-11-10 (paywalled; the owner supplied the text).

**The article's argument, briefly.** World models are AI that predicts the next
*visual* state of the world the way a language model predicts the next word, and
generates simulated 3D environments that "look and feel real." They are mostly for
training robots and self-driving cars on millions of simulated runs. Games are a
side note (Tencent is investing). The money is large: Yann LeCun's new startup
raised over a billion dollars; AMD bought Fei-Fei Li's World Labs for over $8
billion. Its two framing stories: **Niantic** sold Pokémon Go and pivoted to world
models built from what its players scanned, and **the data gig economy**: the
reporter strapped an iPhone to his forehead and sold video of his own chores.

**The quotes worth keeping** (verbatim, attributed as in the article):

- Shotton: *"Importantly, a world model must be able to react to the actions that
  we take in the world, and so it must understand physics, cause and effect, and how
  other objects and people change their actions in response to our own."*
- Cameron: *"With a world model, you give it a visual state of the world, an image,
  and it will then predict the next visual state of the world."* And: *"They're
  simulations, but they look and feel real."*
- McClendon: *"We're very much about reconstructing the real world."*
- The article, to the 2016 Pokémon Go player: *"What you don't realize is that these
  scans of your environment will one day help robots navigate obstacles and interact
  with objects."*
- The reporter, on recording his chores: *"selling the video data through an app for
  a fairly measly payout. As more companies attempt to build better world models,
  even more gig work may demand this kind of data collection."*
- The close: *"these systems are ingesting more and more of my physical life."*

**How each lands on Saxonberg:**

| The article says | Saxonberg |
|---|---|
| Shotton: a world model must understand physics, cause and effect, and how people respond | Our design brief, word for word. The difference is method (below) |
| Cameron: predict the next frame; it should *look and feel real* | We derive, not predict: every outcome traces to a rule a player can learn and the polity can amend (lens 1, a derivable world) |
| Niantic: players' play became robot training data, unknown to them | The measurement doctrine: *the feed hides the measurement; the mirror shows you* ([measurement.md](./measurement.md)); the sponsorship guardrail: no player data is sold, ever ([sponsorship-slate](./slates/builds/sponsorship-slate.md) § 3) |
| Data gig work at a measly payout | Labor standing: contribution recorded and turned into voice ([influence.md](./subsystems/influence.md)); *labor, not producer* |

**⭐⭐ Our response.** *Their world models optimize for looking real; ours is built
to be explainable.* A model that predicts the next frame cannot tell you why the
cup fell; a world built from rules can, and in Saxonberg the players can change
the rules. That is the line between a simulation you are trained by and one you
learn from and govern.

**⭐ The Niantic lesson, which is our answer to it.** Pokémon Go turned its players'
walks into robot training data, and nobody asked them. In Saxonberg nothing is done
with what players do except in the open, and what is done with it is the
players' decision.

**⚠ An open tension, recorded, not resolved.** Saxonberg's event ledgers (every
act, its cause, its outcome) are exactly the structured cause-and-effect data these
labs pay gig workers to fake with forehead cameras. Someone may one day offer to
buy it. The standing guardrail says never sold; a polity could someday *choose* to
license it, with contributors earning standing for it. Whether that choice belongs
to the Compact, or is ruled out by the measurement doctrine's unamendable list, is
undecided.

**Copy seeds** (drafts):

> Pokémon Go turned your walks into robot training data, and nobody asked you.
> Here, the players decide.

> Their world models are built to look real. Ours is built to be explained, and
> changed by the people who live in it.
