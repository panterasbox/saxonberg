## Narration (draft — plain explainer)

**[1 — why trust the executive?]**
The executive writes the code — and the code is what generates the record. Every
vote counted, every law enacted, every verdict entered: a program the executive
wrote produced that entry and wrote it down. So the branch that builds the world
also holds the pen that writes its history. That's a lot of power in one place, and
it raises the obvious question: what stops them from writing code that lies? Cooking
a vote count, logging a rule nobody passed, quietly editing a verdict after the fact?

**[2 — the record is reality]**
It's a sharper problem than it is in the physical world, because a digital world has
no outside. When a country needs to know what happened, it can check against the
world: witnesses, paper trails, footage, the building itself. Its records only
describe a reality that exists apart from them. Here there is no apart. The vote
didn't happen and then get written down — the writing-down is the vote. The history
of this place isn't stored in the record; it *is* the record. A stream of ones and
zeros is all there is. So whoever controls that record doesn't just control the
story of what happened — they control what happened. Hand it to one trusted
authority and you haven't built a government; you've handed someone the power to
rewrite reality.

**[3 — don't trust, verify]**
So the record is built so it can't be faked in secret. Change a single past entry —
anyone, including the people running the place — and the whole record visibly
breaks. And the code that writes the record sits under that same record: the executive
can rewrite it, but the rewrite is itself a logged, visible entry. There's no editing
the history from outside, because there is no outside. And the machinery isn't a black
box either: every line of code that runs
here is open to read, so what you can check isn't only the numbers — it's the machine
that produced them. So you don't take their word that the vote was sixty-forty; you
check it yourself, from the raw record, and either get sixty-forty or catch that you
don't. Don't trust; verify.

**[4 — the one useful property, none of the rest]**
This idea already has a name: it's a blockchain — the data structure, not the
cryptocurrency built on top of it. We take the one property that matters — an
append-only record no one can secretly rewrite, that anyone can independently check —
and none of the rest. No coin, no token, nothing to trade. Just the verifiability,
doing the one thing it's genuinely good at: making trust unnecessary.

**[5 — independent of its keeper]**
So the record's honesty doesn't rest on trusting the executive — it rests on math.
Even the branch that holds the pen can't quietly change what it already wrote, which
makes tampering with the record the one unforgivable crime: nothing — no vote, no
verdict, no law — can stand on a faked one.

**[6 — the honest limit: detectable, not impossible]**
Everything so far binds the executive *inside* the system — the branch that writes
the code can't cheat the record it keeps. But the whole system runs on a machine
somebody owns, and that operator sits *outside* it, with one power no in-system
branch has: they can pull the plug. But none of this makes cheating impossible. A
determined operator could still delete the record, hide it, or run their own forked
copy. What they can't do is any of it in secret — tampering is always visible. So the
guarantee isn't "they can't," it's "they can't get away with it." And when you can see
someone cheating, you have the last word: you take your things and leave — fork off,
somewhere honest. A liar gets
caught; a caught liar gets abandoned. That's the floor under everything.

**[7 — hand forward]**
So you don't have to trust whoever runs this; you can check every number yourself, and
walk if they cheat. That covers the community — the people who live here. The last
chapter flips sides: it's for the person who wants to *stand one of these up.* How do
you switch on a whole constitution — three chambers, courts, the works — without
handing the keys to a crowd that doesn't exist yet? That's the last chapter.

## Visual cue sheet

Corner default; **no-face** for the reality contrast + the chain hero. Client never
shown. (Carried from `chapter-5.md`.)

- **1 — corner.** The executive (from Ch 5) at a keyboard: the **code** it writes
  feeds a printer spooling out **the record** (votes, laws, verdicts as entries). The
  branch that writes the world also holds the pen that writes its history — a question
  mark: *what stops them writing code that lies?*
- **2 — no-face.** The contrast: a real-world side (witnesses, a building, a paper
  trail) vs. the digital side — **only a stream of 1s and 0s, no outside.** Then:
  control of the stream = control of *what happened.*
- **3 — the hero, no-face.** A **tamper-evident chain** of entries; alter one past
  block and the whole chain **visibly breaks** (a red mismatch propagates). Then
  "check it yourself": raw record → re-derive **60–40**.
- **4 — no-face.** A **blockchain** labeled plainly as a data structure (linked
  entries); the **cryptocurrency layer** bolted on top (coins / a token / a price
  chart) lifts away and is set aside — the chain itself stays. *The property, not the
  currency.*
- **5 — no-face.** The executive editing the record-writing **code** — and the edit
  drops into the record as its own logged entry; the past entries stay **sealed by
  math**, a lock even the one holding the pen can't pick.
- **6 — no-face.** Zoom out: the whole system is a box *inside* a bigger machine an
  **operator** owns (hand on the power switch, outside the box). The operator deletes
  / hides / forks the record — and an **alarm lights up** (always visible); → a member
  **forks away** with their stuff. *Detectable, not impossible.*
- **7 — corner.** Hand-forward: the community can *verify and leave* (✓); now the
  audience **flips to the organizer** standing one up → the dial (Ch 7).
