## Narration (draft — plain explainer)

**[1 — origin, then the problem]**
It begins with something ordinary: online, people already govern themselves. From the
early internet's text worlds to today's livestreams and Discord servers, they make their
own rules and keep their own order with nothing but each other. Self-organizing isn't rare
online — it's constant. Where it breaks down is turning it into real self-government:
getting people to take part in the deciding, not just the hanging out.

**[2 — a game makes you care: investment]**
A game is the rare thing that pulls people in without being asked — through **investment.**
You sink hours into a character: advancing it, equipping it, earning a reputation. That
effort is what makes it matter — it's all just data, but it's data you *earned*, so what
happens to it lands on you like it's real. And once your place in a world matters to you,
so do the rules that shape it — you're drawn into the governing without ever being asked.
In civic life, taking part is a cost; in a game, it's the reward.

**[3 — the graveyard]**
That missing piece is what sank the earlier attempts. The online democracies, the
DAOs, the token-voting experiments built the machinery of deciding — ballots,
rules, tallies — and waited for people who never came. They didn't fail because the
ideas were bad; they failed because nobody cared enough to use them. They had the
voting and not the participation.

**[4 — unity of substance]**
A digital world adds one more thing. Everything in it is the same kind of stuff —
the argument, the vote, the law, the code that enforces the law, the record of all
of it — all just words. Information. And information is legible, checkable, and
copyable in a way paper ballots and stone buildings are not: recorded exactly,
verifiable by anyone, and forkable by anyone who wants their own. A polity made of
words is one you can inspect, verify, and carry off.

**[5 — close: the participation engine, and a laboratory]**
So the game is the source of the one thing self-government can't manufacture on its
own: people who care enough to show up. And it gives one more thing. Because the world
underneath is modeled honestly — real scarcity, real trade, real commons, real costs
that land on people who didn't choose them — the questions this government has to
answer are the real ones. Do we tax what comes out of the ground? Who pays for the
thing everyone uses and nobody wants to fund? What happens when one group's freedom is
another group's cost? Those aren't game problems in political costume; they're the
problems every real government faces — just small enough to see all the way through,
and fast enough to watch play out. That makes it a laboratory: not a model *of* a
government, a place to *run* one — take an idea about how people should decide
together, build it into a working world, and watch how it holds up. Real politics
rarely lets you do that; you get one shot, at enormous scale, no rewind. Here you can
try it, watch it break, and try again. And nothing here is specific to games — a game is
just a community whose life is already, entirely, data, which is exactly what lets you run
this at all. Whatever part of a community can be turned into data, this can govern — in
the open, every rule readable by the people it binds. And that part only grows as the
technology does: it never leaves the digital, the digital just keeps taking in more.

That's the case for the game. What it doesn't answer is how the governing itself works —
starting with the simplest question of all: who even gets a say? That's next.
