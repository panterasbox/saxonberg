## Chapter 1 — The whole machine, end to end

**Title:** `The Whole Machine, End to End — A Constitution for Online Communities (Ch 1)`

---
A game with a government built into it — and a machine any online community can pick up and run in the open.

This is the whole system in one pass. A proposal starts on an open floor where anyone can put one down. It gets argued on a map that organizes claims by how they answer each other rather than by who's loudest, where an objection nobody has answered stays lit until someone answers it. Then three co-equal chambers vote — labor, capital, and consumers — and nothing passes by capturing just one. Three branches ship it the way a software team ships: the legislature writes requirements, the executive builds the code and runs the world, the judiciary reviews the build against the law. Every step lands in a record anyone can re-derive for themselves.

None of it is all-or-nothing. It runs on a dial, from one operator deciding everything to a full republic — set wherever a community trusts itself, moved as fast as its founder chooses.

The rest of the series is this machine slowed down: one piece per video, each taking on the hardest question about it. Watch in order, or go straight to the part you want to see up close.
---

## Chapter 2 — Why a game

**Title:** `Why a Game — A Constitution for Online Communities (Ch 2)`

---
A game is the participation engine self-government never had.

People organize themselves online constantly — a chat that polices itself, a subreddit that writes its own rules, a Discord that grows a whole culture of mods and norms nobody handed down. So why is deliberate self-government still the exception? Not for lack of tools. What's scarce is participation: enough people willing to do the work of deciding, not just enjoy a place someone else runs.

A game supplies exactly that, through investment. You sink hours into a character, and because the effort was yours, what happens to it lands on you like it's real — and so do the rules that shape it. In civic life taking part is a cost; in a game, it's the reward. That missing piece is what sank the earlier attempts: the online democracies and the token-voting experiments built the ballots and the tallies and waited for people who never came. They had the voting and not the participation.

A digital world adds one more thing. The argument, the vote, the law, the code that enforces the law, and the record of all of it are the same kind of stuff — words. A polity made of words is one you can inspect, verify, and carry off.

## Chapter 3 — The Chambers: Earning a Voice

**Title:** `The Chambers: Earning a Voice — A Constitution for Online Communities (Ch 3)`

---
Every economy runs on three forces — labor, capital, and the consumer. Only two of them have ever had a way to organize.

Labor built unions. Capital was born with boards and shareholders. The consumer, the party the whole thing exists to serve, has only ever had exit — buy it or don't — never voice. Real-time communication at scale is the first thing that can turn a hundred million private decisions into a standing seat, and seating the third force ends a very old two-body deadlock.

So each force gets its own count — its own chamber — and the three are co-equal: a decision needs two of the three, and none can outvote the other two. They aren't three tribes. Everyone makes, funds, and plays in some proportion, so a chamber isn't a room full of different people; it's the same crowd measured by what they contribute.

Then the part everyone watches: money. Funding the place earns a real voice, because that's how the lights stay on — but only in capital's own chamber. Never a vote among the makers or the players, never an edge out in the world. This video covers what a voice is actually made of: standing as a product of quantity and quality, conferred by other people rather than minted by you, and spent as conviction without ever being spent down.
---

## Chapter 4 — The Argument Map: Not the Crowd

**Title:** `The Argument Map: Not the Crowd — A Constitution for Online Communities (Ch 4)`

---
A real decision is organized by the structure of the argument — never by who's loudest.

Online conversation splits two ways. Chat is live, the forum is posted and builds over time; and either can be a loose feed where popular things rise, or structured, where every point attaches to the argument at a specific place. Casual talk can stay loose. Decisions move to the structured forms.

The model is one that already arrives in your mail: the voter guide. The proposal, the case for, the case against, the rebuttals — laid out by structure rather than volume, and delivered separately from the ballot. The difference here is that it's interactive. Instead of a pamphlet a few officials wrote, it's a living map the whole community builds, where every point attaches as a support, an objection, or a question. No upvotes and no ranking.

The map is also blind to who you are, so an objection from an unknown carries the same weight as one from the most prominent name in the room. And because there's no voting, dissent can't be buried: an unanswered objection stays flagged as open until someone answers its strongest form. That's the one quantity the map tracks — which makes it ungameable, because reducing it and improving the argument are the same action.

## Chapter 5 — Governing is shipping software

**Title:** `Governing Is Shipping Software — A Constitution for Online Communities (Ch 5)`

---
A decision is just words until something changes. Turning one into a running system is what a government's branches do — and here they line up like building software.

Decide, build, check. The legislature decides, and what it writes is requirements: the what and the why, never the how. The executive takes those requirements and makes them real — it builds the world, runs it live, and holds the human administration of the place. The judiciary reads the build and checks it against the law.

The executive has one power no real government has. Every government enforces through officers and agencies, always after the fact; here the world itself is made of code, so a law can be written into it and simply hold. You can't walk through a wall. You can't spend money you don't have. Nothing catches you afterward — it just can't happen. Hence the maxim: what can be enforced by code, shall be enforced by code, because a machine enforces a rule the same way for everyone, every time, with no discretion to bend and no official to bribe.

But not every rule reduces to code. Setting monetary policy, shutting down a new exploit, handling a crisis no rule anticipated — that work stays human, bounded by law and open to review. This video covers the split, who gets to write code and who decides that, and why the judiciary's juries are drawn by lot with reputation counting for nothing.
---

## Chapter 6 — The Record: Don't Trust, Verify

**Title:** `The Record: Don't Trust, Verify — A Constitution for Online Communities (Ch 6)`

---
The branch that builds the world also holds the pen that writes its history. So the record is built so that even they can't quietly change it.

Every vote counted, every law enacted, every verdict entered — a program the executive wrote produced that entry. That's a lot of power in one place, and it's sharper here than in the physical world, because a digital world has no outside. A country can check its records against witnesses, paper, footage, the building itself. Here there is no apart: the vote didn't happen and then get written down — the writing-down is the vote. Whoever controls the record doesn't just control the story of what happened. They control what happened.

So change a single past entry — anyone, including the people running the place — and the whole record visibly breaks. The code that writes the record sits under that same record, so a rewrite is itself a logged, visible entry. And every line of code that runs here is open to read, which means what you can check isn't only the numbers; it's the machine that produced them.

The idea already has a name: it's a blockchain — the data structure, not the cryptocurrency built on top of it. Take the one property that matters, an append-only record no one can secretly rewrite that anyone can independently check, and none of the rest. No coin, no token, nothing to trade. This video also covers the honest limit — where tamper-evidence stops, and what the real backstop is.

## Chapter 7 — The Dial: Operator to Republic

**Title:** `The Dial: Operator to Republic — A Constitution for Online Communities (Ch 7)`

---
This one's for the person starting a community, not joining one. You do not switch on a republic and hand the keys to a crowd that doesn't exist yet.

The same process runs at any size, a population of one or a million. What changes is only how much the community drives and how much you do — and that's the dial, from "you decide everything" to "the community decides everything," turned on your own schedule.

Start with the part that doesn't move. Wherever the dial is set, three things are guaranteed and not even you can switch them off: money can never buy power, the record can never be faked, and anyone can always leave — walking out with their account, their standing, and everything they built. That's the floor the dial sits on.

Everything above it is yours to set. And here's the part worth sitting with: you're already running a government. You just call it moderation. The operator is the executive, the mods are the police, the unwritten rules are the law, the ban appeals are the courts, the mod-log is the record. This doesn't drop a government onto a blank slate; it formalizes the one every community already has — and rule-of-law is a gift to whoever runs the place, because it lets the rules take the heat instead of you. This video also covers where a founder's control actually comes from, and why it isn't a carve-out.
---

## Chapter stops

**Recommendation: none, on six of the seven.**

These are single-argument explainers where each beat loads the next — Ch 2's
payoff is in its fifth beat, Ch 4's in its fourth. Chapters serve skimming, and
a viewer who jumps mid-way gets a conclusion without its setup. Under about six
minutes the chapter UI also segments the progress bar for navigation nobody
needs.

The structural reason matters more than either: the 2026-07-15 format decision
already split one video with chapter stops into **one video per chapter in a
playlist**. The playlist *is* the chapter system. Adding chapters inside each
video puts back the thing that was deliberately taken out.

**The exception is Chapter 1**, if it runs 5:00 or longer. Its closing line is
*"watch in order, or go straight to the part you want to see up close,"* and its
six beats map one-to-one onto the six later videos — so chapters there aren't
navigation, they're a table of contents for the series. Paste this into the
description with real timestamps (YouTube requires the first at 0:00, at least
three, each 10s or longer):

```
0:00 Why this exists
0:00 What it is — a game, and a government
0:00 A proposal
0:00 It gets argued
0:00 Three chambers decide
0:00 Three branches build it
0:00 All of it is written down
0:00 The dial — operator to republic
```

**Working rule for anything else in the series** (the appendices, if they get
cut): chapter it only at 8:00 or longer. Appendix A and B are the likeliest to
qualify — both are descriptive catalogues rather than arguments, which is
exactly the material people scrub through.

## Thumbnails

Seven at `exports/thumbnails/ch{1-7}-thumb.png` — 1280×720, ~90–105 KB, well
inside YouTube's 2 MB limit. Regenerate with `python3 thumbnail-generator.py`.

The set is deliberately identical apart from number and title, so it reads as a
series in a playlist sidebar. Two decisions worth keeping if they're ever
redone:

- **The ground is Old Glory Blue `#002868`** — the same value the rings mark
  hard-codes as its casing, which is what `docs/brand/README.md` requires ("the
  casing colour must match whatever is behind the mark").
- **The rings are cut from `background.png`, not from
  `saxonberg-rings-white.svg`.** Both are correct Borromean, but the SVG's
  casing is 2.55× its ink stroke — at thumbnail scale that opens the crossings
  wide enough to read as three broken arcs. The flag's proportions survive the
  downscale; the vector's don't.

Titles are split on the em-dash where the chapter title has one, so the second
half — the part that actually carries the idea — gets the large line.
