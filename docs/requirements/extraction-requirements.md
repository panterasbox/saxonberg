# Extraction — requirements

**Kind:** feature
**Leads from:** content — a new trade and its workings lead, over three
kernel prerequisites (W0 the drying rate · the `dig` verb · promoting ground
improvement). ⭐ **First consumer of each kernel piece, named:** the drying
rate is exercised by the turf stack and the salt pan *in this build*; `dig` by
the clay face and the turbary *in this build*; the promoted improvement acts by
draining a turbary *in this build*. Nothing kernel ships here unexercised.

Executes [extraction-slate](../slates/builds/extraction-slate.md) — the last
civilized RGO. Six shipped RGOs take what is planted, kept, standing, sunk,
mined underground and carried by water; **nothing cuts the ground from the
surface**, and a census found six goods the world consumes and nobody makes.
This build closes them, and with the bees wave on ranching it closes the
primary sector, which is the precondition the user set before foraging and
hunting.

## What already exists

### Verbs near this

Six acts win something out of the ground, all underground or standing:
mining's **`hew`** (a face), **`drive`/`sink`/`raise`** (new workings),
**`shore`** (timber), **`stake`** (a claim at the counter); forestry's
**`fell`** (a standard, then the bole a length at a time); fuel's **`char`**.
Farming improves ground rather than taking it: **`grub`** (thorn, root and
stone), **`ditch`**, **`plough`**, **`lime`**, **`plot`**, **`mow`**.

⚠ **There is no `dig`, `strip`, `quarry`, `pare`, `delve` or turf-cutting verb
anywhere.** `cut` exists and is **tailoring's** (cloth). `dig` was designed and
withdrawn during the fishing build — its shape is recorded in
[discovery-slate](../slates/builds/discovery-slate.md) and its view and
controller are in MR !268's history, kept deliberately *"if the shape is wanted
back."*

### Trades and packs

21 `trade-*` packs; **none covers surface extraction.** `trade-mining` owns the
underground shape, and a hand-authored mine already works without the warren
(Rejection ships both layers side by side), which is the precedent a quarry
follows.

### Disciplines

68 rows. **None** for quarrying, masonry, ceramics, glasswork, salt or
beekeeping. `mining` (0724) and `geology` (0532) are the nearest.

### Substrate that ships and is unused

- ⭐⭐ **A kiln already exists and stands nowhere.** Both the thing and a
  ready-made row for it ship — a steady 1200 K, hotter with bellows — and the
  row describes itself as *"a ceramics / lime firing furnace."* **Nothing in the
  world references it**: it is in no place, no recipe and no boot list.
- ⭐ **The miner's pick already declares that it wins stone, and no act asks.**
  The declaration satisfies a kit list that is *reported, never enforced* — so
  today `hew` works bare-handed.
- **Digging is already a thing tools declare** — the farm spade and the mine
  shovel both offer it, and four shipped acts ask for it. So a person may
  already own the tool the earths need.
- **`quicklime` ships as a material** with no limestone and no kiln.
  **`salt-water`** ships. **`organic/ash`** ships as a near-unused byproduct.
- `granite` (13 consumer sites) and `slate` (10) ship and **nothing produces
  them**; `ceramic` (9 sites) ships and **no `clay` material exists at all**.

### Overlapping slates — checked, and none is a second owner

`mining-slate` stakes *"Quarrying (stone, flux) — open-pit bulk"* and
*"Evaporation / solution (salt) — coastal pans, brine-springs, rock-salt"* on
its **Left** and cedes them. `ranching-slate` carries the saltern, peat and bog
as **inherited acceptance criteria (AC 37–39)**, not a design.
`metal-chain-slate` owns **coal → coke** and parks it as the arc payload.
`content-packs-slate` lists `quarrying` on its unbuilt-trades roster.
`end-of-life-slate` wants `masonry` minted, which this build defers.

> **Therefore what is genuinely new here is: one act that wins rock off a
> face, one act that digs earth, and a drying rate that reads the air.**
> Everything else is a shipped fixture being wired, a shipped capability being
> given its first consumer, or rows.

## Goals

- **Ground that is cut from the surface is a place a person can work**, with
  overburden that must come off before the good comes out — and the ratio of
  the two is what decides whether the working is worth anything.
- **Six goods have honest origins**: building stone, clay, limestone, coal,
  peat and salt. Every one has a consumer that already exists and is today fed
  by an authored fiction.
- **Salt has all three of its real sources** — a face you cut, pans the sun
  works, and brine you boil with fuel somebody else wants — so where a person
  lives decides how they get it.
- **Drying is a rate that reads the air**, so a thing left out dries at a speed
  the weather sets and gets wet again when it rains; and the preserving trades
  sell *control of the air*, not the only route to dry.
- **A cut turbary does not come back**, and draining one destroys the fuel
  while making the best arable ground there is — a choice with no dominant
  option, available to anybody who holds the ground upstream.
- **A second working needs no code** — a row for what the ground holds and a
  row for the place.
- **Limestone closes the smelt's missing flux**, and clay closes `ceramic`.
- **The quarrying Discipline exists**, is exercised by working stone, and makes
  the read of a face sharper as it rises.

- **Bees, honey, beeswax and pollination** → a **ranching wave**.
  `ranching.md § ⚠ Not built: bees (D34–D39)` already carries the design, calls
  it *severable by construction*, ships `Apis mellifera` and clover, and names

## Placement

**A new capability pack, `trade-quarrying`, rooted at `/trade/quarrying`** —
holding the working, the two acts, the Discipline, the tools and the pit's
rows. It answers the `/trade/` axis question (*a trade is practised by somebody
and can be quit*), and the second-instance test passes: a second pit is a row
for what the ground holds plus a row for the place.

**Three pieces go to the kernel and the platform, each because more than one
trade needs them:**

- **The drying rate** — the kernel's maturation substrate already declares the
  extension point this needs. Consumers in this build: the turf stack and the
  salt pan. Consumers already shipped and waiting: the drying rack, the smoke
  chimney, the salting trough. Consumer next door: haymaking.
- **`dig`** — the platform's, conferred by the instrument, with the ground as
  the argument. Two trades want it (this one now, foraging next) and it does not
  exist yet, so the honest home costs nothing. A verb in a trade pack would mean
  only that trade's ground could ever be dug, which fails the author test.
- **Ground improvement** — promoted out of farming. You ditch a road, a yard
  and a quarry; improvement acts on **ground**, and ground's derived half is
  already the kernel's, so farming is improvement's first consumer and not its
  owner. This is what lets a turbary be drained.

**The materials go to the commons, not the trade** — clay and limestone join
the granite and slate already there. **Clay exists whether or not anybody digs
it**, which is the rule fishing stated for a trout.

**The Kiln stays where it is** — a generic fixture the world already owns. This
build places one at the quarry, because you burn lime where you dig the stone:
the rock is heavy and the lime is light, and that is the whole reason limekilns
stand at quarries.

## Collisions

### Rejection — the resource town, and it is already describing this

The industrial locality: a mine, a wood, a smelter, a fuel yard, a claims
office, an assay shed, a spoil bank, eight named people and five businesses.

- ⭐⭐ **`old-workings` already has building stone in its prose**: *"A quarter-
  acre of hillside that has been turned over and left… **Somebody built a wall
  out of the bigger pieces and then stopped building it.**"* The fiction is
  there and the object is not.
- **`kestrel-road/tips` is already the spoil bank** — its own keywords are
  `spoil, waste, bank`. Overburden has somewhere to go that was authored
  before this build existed.
- **The claims office and `stake` already exist**, so the pit is claimed
  through the counter people already use, not a second mechanism.
- **The smelter is a five-minute cart away**, which is what makes limestone a
  flux rather than a curiosity.
- ⚠ **The independent, the hewer, the collier and the buyer already live
  here.** The pit must read as another way to work the same hill, not as a
  second town.

### The Weeping Moor — peat is already in the prose too

⭐ `heath-mere` is *"a mere of **black peat-water**, rain-pocked and
wind-scoured."* Five rooms, open to the sky, storm-lashed — a turbary belongs
here and the landscape has been saying so. ⚠ The mere carries a fishing
**reach**, so draining ground here touches water somebody fishes.

### The estuary — where the pans go

Three rooms, tidal, and **`salt-water` already ships as a material**. The pans
and the brine house sit here; the rock-salt face sits in the inland pit, which
is what makes coastal patience and inland capital different choices.

### Farming — two real overlaps

- ⚠ **`grub` already turns out stone and marl on limy ground**, and a
  field-stone already ships. Building stone must not duplicate it: what a pit
  yields is a block too heavy to lift and rubble, not another field stone.
- ⚠ **`marl` is already soil's no-kiln pH lever** — and ⭐⭐ **the first version
  of this line was wrong.** It said limestone must never lime a field, so the
  kiln could not become a worse marl. **Corrected 2026-09-23 against the
  lenses:** in the world **quicklime IS agricultural lime**, faster and more
  aggressive than marl, and `soil.md` already ships the seam for it (`lime`
  reads a `liming` tag *"so a kiln's output works the day somebody ships one"*).
  So quicklime carries the tag, and marl and quicklime become **a choice rather
  than a rule** — marl is slow and needs no fire, quicklime is fast and burnt.
  Lens 1 decided it; lens 6 confirms marl survives as the fuel-free path.

### Cooking's three preserving fixtures

The drying rack, the salting trough and the smoke chimney ship, and today
**none of them has any craft in it** — each is a flat constant. The drying rate
gives all three a real failure mode. ⚠ **Ham behaviour changes**, and that is
the intent: hanging a ham in a damp cellar currently dries it exactly as well
as an August wind does.

### Mining's own act

⚠ **`hew` requires no tool.** The pick says it wins stone; the act never asks.
This build is the first thing to actually ask, so `hew` is corrected in the same
breath: a tool that is not required is a number with no referent.

## Surface decisions

### A working is a place, and it does not grow by adding rooms

A mine grows a graph of headings because **navigation is the hazard** — air,
support, water. A quarry does not: **the face retreats and the floor drops.**
One place changing, not new places appearing. The shipped doctrine already
separates the two (*reads go to the space, mutation goes to the warren*), and a
hand-authored working with no warren is already a shipped class. When a pit is
deep enough that a bench is genuinely a heading, that is a later promotion.

### Overburden is the gate, and it is the lesson

You strip waste before you win good, and how much waste lies over how much good
is what decides whether the ground is worth working. ⭐ **This is the first RGO
in the game whose cost is what you throw away** — and it is the whole of
extractive economics, which nothing else teaches.

### Two acts, not one, and each names its tool

**`dig`** takes earth — clay, turf, and overburden — and wants the digging
capability a spade already offers. **`quarry`** wins rock off a face and asks for
stone-winning, which the pick already claims and nothing has ever checked. Two acts because
rock and earth genuinely take different tools, and a single act would hide
that. A second pass of `quarry` on a won block splits a piece off it at a time,
which is the shape felling already uses for a bole.

### A block cannot be lifted, and the refusal says why

What comes off a stone face is too heavy for a body, exactly as a felled trunk
is. The refusal must read as *this weighs more than you can lift* and never as a
flag — bigness is emergent from mass.

### Drying is a rate, and the doctrine's prohibition is lifted with a reason

Today drying is instantaneous and flat: the same amount of water leaves a ham
whatever the weather, and the engine models only the *re-wetting* half against
ambient humidity. That asymmetry is a lookup table dressed as physics — a
player who understands evaporation cannot predict it.

The standing rule says nothing may dry on its own, for fear of *"quietly
preserving every ration in the pantry."* Run the numbers the engine already
ships and the fear resolves itself: equilibrium moisture is ambient humidity and
the microbial floor sits at 0.60 water activity, so **passive drying only
crosses the floor below roughly 62% ambient humidity.** A damp cellar preserves
nothing; a dry loft preserves slowly; an arid place preserves well. All three
are correct, and the doc already wants a dry store to be worth building. So the
rule is lifted, and **the preserving trades' product becomes making air drier
than the weather** — a fire, a kiln, salt — rather than being the only way to
remove water.

### Salt has three sources because it really does

A face you cut (capital and labour) · pans the sun works (patience, and rain
sets you back) · brine you boil (fast, and the fuel is what the charcoal burner
and the hearth also want). One good, three cost structures, and which one a
person uses is decided by where they live. The fuel-competition leg was already
designed as part of the wood contest.

### A turbary does not come back, and it can be destroyed without being touched

Peat is ground that is nearly all organic matter with a finite depth. Cutting
removes depth. **Draining** oxidises and subsides it — you lose the fuel and
gain the best arable ground there is. ⭐⭐ So peat carries an irreversible choice
with no dominant option — **the fuel or the field, not both** — and whoever
drains upstream destroys a neighbour's turbary without ever entering it. This
is why ground improvement is promoted: without drainage, peat is a fuel with no
lesson.

### Coal ships as a domestic fuel, not an iron fuel

Raw coal in a bloomery makes bad iron — sulfur is exactly why coke had to be
invented. So coal relieves the hearth and the kiln, iron keeps needing
charcoal, and the energy transition stays available to be told as the epoch
step it is.

### Limestone's sink is the flux, and the kiln is the second product

Limestone goes straight into the smelt as flux — a named hole in the metal
chain. Through the kiln it becomes quicklime, which costs fuel and is more
reactive. Two products from one rock, one of which needs a fire: the kiln's
price is the lesson.

### `quarrying` is its own Discipline

Every RGO trade ships its own, and surface and underground are genuinely
different competence — the masons, potters and lime burners who arrive later
will all read the surface one. ⚠ The `extraction` key that an older roster
lists **was never minted**, so there is nothing for this to be a band of, and
the ISCED-F code must be checked against a source before seeding.

## Lens pass

**1 · Pedagogy.** Exercises **`quarrying`** (new), and `geology` and `mining`
where they already read ground. Three lessons no shipped RGO teaches: **the
waste ratio** — the first cost in the game that is what you discard;
**depletion on a human timescale** — the turbary runs out and does not return;
and **drying as a rate**, where the air's dryness, not a recipe, decides how
fast water leaves. All derivable: a player who knows that evaporation needs a
vapour deficit predicts the drying shed, the damp cellar and the rainy week
correctly without looking anything up. ⭐ And two instances of one principle —
a flux lowers a melting point — since limestone in the smelt and (later) ash in
glass are the same idea twice.

**2 · Creative expression.** The ordinary case is **two rows**: what the ground
holds and where the place is. `dig` in the platform means any author can make
ground diggable with no pack code at all. The bespoke case overrides the
authored band, exactly as the existing deposit and ground-character fields
already permit. ⭐ A second pit needs zero pack code.

**3 · Immersion & roleplay.** A face reads in words and gets more precise as
competence rises; turves read wet and read drier after a windy day and wetter
after rain. The drying season becomes playable — *rain is a disaster* is
unbuildable while drying is a constant. Nothing is a gauge and nothing needs
scripting.

**4 · Values.** ⭐ **The fuel or the field** is a real choice with no dominant
option and no villain — and the externality runs downhill, so somebody
improving their own ground in good faith can ruin a neighbour's. Turbary and
quarry rights are classic rights to take from land somebody else holds, which
the existing title record can already express. Standing here is conferred by the
trade that buys from you: the smelter that wants your flux, the household that
wants your turf.

**5 · Technology & magic.** An open pit is an open pit from Rome to the Rand;
blasting, draglines and a rope shovel change the rate, never the mechanism.
Drying holds from a turf stack to a kiln to a dehumidifier. Coal's arrival *is*
the epoch change, which is why it is staged rather than assumed.

**6 · Economy.** ⭐⭐ **The demand is not merely prior — it is live and being
fed a fiction.** Salt is consumed by a working preserve act and comes from a
sack that came from nowhere; `ceramic` is used at nine sites with no clay in
existence; granite and slate at 13 and 10 with no producer; the whole fuel
chain roots on wood alone. Produces: six goods. Consumes: time, a tool, and the
waste you move for nothing. Who pays: the smelter for flux, the household for
turf, the cook for salt, and the mason when that trade lands. Nobody's
obligation was invented to make any of it wanted.

⭐⭐ **Who can be wronged, on what basis, and can they answer?** (Lens 6's
governance limb, added to the rubric after this doc was first written — and it
lands hard here.) **The turbary is the case.** Whoever drains the ground upslope
destroys a neighbour's fuel without entering their land, and **today the neighbour
cannot answer**: there is no mechanism that couples one room's ditch to another's
water, so the wrong is real in the fiction and absent from the model. The limb
makes that gap sharper than "AC 12 is met weakly" did, and names its home — the
watershed's rights, where prior appropriation is already recorded and an
appropriation can already be contested. ⚠ **Do not paper it over with a refusal**:
forbidding the ditch would invent a criterion nobody chose. The honest ship is the
externality without the remedy, *said out loud*, and the remedy owed to the
watershed. The other refusals this build adds — no tool, still buried, no face
above you — are physical, stated in words, and wrong nobody.

**Gap, recorded as a gap:** the consumers of building stone are all *authored
presets* — a wall, a whetstone, a bowl. Until masonry ships, stone's only live
buyer is whoever wants the prose to be true. That is the weakest leg of lens 6
in this build, and it is why masonry is named as the next trade rather than a
someday.

## The drive

Run against the running game before the MR opens.

1. Log in, go to Rejection, and walk to the new pit above the old workings.
2. `look` — the read names the rock, says that waste lies over it, and gives a
   vague quality word. A character with no `quarrying` gets the vague version.
3. `quarry` with empty hands → refused, in words that name what you need to
   win stone with.
4. Take up a pick. `quarry` again → refused **because of the overburden**, and
   the refusal says so.
5. `dig` with a spade → a load of overburden comes off and goes to the spoil.
   Repeat until the read says the face is open.
6. `quarry` the stone face → a block on the floor. `get` it → refused because
   of what it weighs.
7. `quarry` the block → one piece splits off, carryable. Take it.
8. `quarry` the limestone face → limestone. Carry it to the smelter and `smelt`
   with ore and charcoal → compare against a smelt without it; the slag and the
   outcome differ.
9. Light the limekiln at the pit, charge it with limestone, fire it → quicklime.
10. `dig` the clay face → clay. Fire it in the kiln → a ceramic pot you can
    hold, plant in, and look at.
11. `quarry` the coal face → coal. Burn it in a hearth → it works. Try to smelt
    iron with it → the iron is worse, and the read says why.
12. `quarry` the rock-salt face → salt. Carry it to a kitchen and `cure` a cut
    of meat with it — the act that until now ran on a sack from nowhere.
13. Go to the estuary. Fill a pan from the tide, leave it in the sun.
14. Come back after a dry windy day → the pan has concentrated. Come back after
    rain → it has gone backwards. Eventually: salt.
15. Boil brine in the salt house instead → salt much faster, and the fuel is
    gone. Note what else wanted that fuel.
16. Go to the Weeping Moor. `dig` turf → wet turves.
17. Stack them. `look` → they read wet. After dry weather → drier. After rain →
    wetter again.
18. Burn a dry turf on a hearth → it works. Burn a wet one → it does not, and
    the refusal says why.
19. `ditch` the turbary. Over days the ground subsides and the peat reads
    thinner. The fuel is going and the ground is improving.
20. Work the same stone face repeatedly → the read says it is thinning. Cut the
    turbary out → it stays cut; nothing brings it back.
21. `competence` → `quarrying` has risen, and step 2's read is now precise.
22. `stake` the pit's ground at the claims office → the record says who holds
    it.
23. In the open pit, try to work the face **above** you → there is no face
    above you. The ground stops at the surface.

## Acceptance criteria

Observable from outside the code, by a person playing.

1. A person can stand on ground that holds stone and be told, **in words**,
   what is there, that waste lies over it, and roughly how good it is — and the
   words get more precise as their quarrying competence rises.
2. Attempting to work a face with the wrong tool, or no tool, is refused in
   words that name what is needed.
3. Overburden must be moved before the good comes out, the moved waste ends up
   somewhere a person can see, and the face's read changes when it is open.
4. A won block cannot be picked up, and the refusal is about its weight.
   Splitting it yields pieces that can be carried.
5. Limestone carried to a smelt visibly changes the outcome versus a smelt
   without it.
6. Clay fired in a kiln becomes a ceramic object a person can hold and use.
7. Coal burns on a hearth; used to smelt iron it produces worse iron, and the
   game says why.
8. Salt can be got three ways — cut from a face, evaporated in a pan, boiled
   from brine — and a person can `cure` meat with salt from any of them.
9. A pan left in the open concentrates faster in dry, windy weather and **goes
   backwards in rain**.
10. Cut turves read wet, dry over days at a speed the weather sets, get wetter
    again when it rains, and only dry ones burn properly.
11. A ham hung in a damp place does **not** dry the way one hung in dry wind
    does.
12. Draining a turbary makes the peat thinner over time and the ground better;
    a person upstream can do this to ground they do not hold.
13. A worked-out face says it is worked out; a cut-over turbary stays cut and
    does not recover.
14. ⚠ **In an open working there is no face above you** — the ground stops at
    the surface, and nothing can be won out of the sky.
15. `quarrying` appears on a transcript after working stone, and nothing but
    doing the work puts it there.
16. The pit's ground can be claimed at the claims office people already use,
    and the record names the holder.
17. A second pit, anywhere, is authored with **no new code** — a row for what
    the ground holds and a row for the place.
