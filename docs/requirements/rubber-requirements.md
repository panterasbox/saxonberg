# Rubber — requirements

**Kind:** feature
**Leads from:** kernel (first consumers ship in this build: the rubber
works and its stove house in the Terminus goods yards, and the Drowned
Substation)

Rubber is the first technology the realm can make that its medieval
baseline cannot: two natural inputs and a controlled heat give a material
neither input is, and the window that gives a good one is narrow enough
that it has to be *measured* rather than felt. The material row has
shipped fully specified since 2026-09 and nothing in the world is made of
it. This build closes the whole chain — where latex grows, where sulfur
is dug, the cure, the goods — and makes the goods matter. Making them
matter turned out to need the world to answer honestly a question it
currently answers with one bit: **what is over you, and what is around
you.** A hothouse is the first place that question has more than two
answers, so it is answered properly here.

## What already exists

- **The material.** `rubber` ships as a fully specified row — insulating,
  elastomer — used by nothing. No latex, no ebonite, no sulfur.
- **The tap.** The tap act ships (`tap`, window predicates that read
  temperature, a vessel that fills as the reservoir); sap is its
  consumer. A latex tap is a species row on the shipped act.
- **No rubber plant can grow here.** No tropical biome, no rubber-bearing
  species; Terminus is a temperate 42° site.
- **Sulfur** exists only as a tag on coal, coal tar and sulfurous iron.
  The Hill Quarry's column runs limestone over rock salt — the geology
  where native sulfur really forms (salt-dome caprock).
- **Heat over time** exists twice and neither fits: the food dose
  (time × temperature, graded raw → burnt) is food-only; the oven's
  `fire` picks a recipe by its charge but ignores how long it is held.
  "The composition decides the material" ships only inside smelting
  (sulfur → sulfurous iron, carbon → steel).
- **Recipe knowledge** (known-of → can-make) ships; nothing gates a
  recipe on the realm's epoch, and nothing reads the epoch stamp.
- **Electricity.** Insulation reads a material's conductivity. Leather
  boots already break the ground path; gloves never do; a soaked garment
  insulates exactly like a dry one.
- **Rain** wets the body directly and never asks what you are wearing.
- **The pump's packing** is leather and wears at one rate whatever it is
  made of.
- **Vitriol** is sold at the general store and is authored to eat
  anything organic — rubber included.
- **Fit.** A garment fits by two numbers; stock clothing is cut to the
  species average; nothing stretches.
- **Sky.** One yes/no — set by a biome row's class — answers five
  different questions (does rain reach me · does the sky light me · can
  I see the sky · is this the outside for heat and air · is the floor
  open ground). The glass-roofed atrium is rained on and struck by
  lightning; a coach's roof keeps out the cold but not the rain.
- **The envelope.** A room's construction is one material on five cube
  faces, all treated as outside; its own doc records that a buried
  corridor loses heat like a shopfront and that subdividing a hall makes
  it colder.
- **Structures** ship — which rooms are one building, its entrance, its
  look from outside, its height — built for the ship and for what is
  seen over the horizon, and authored for few buildings.
- **Glass** ships as a trade; its tint is Beer–Lambert; two authored
  windows exist. The glass material carries no optical property.
- **Room warmth** is derived (walls, openings, what burns inside), and
  growth already stops when it is too cold.

**Therefore what is genuinely new here is:** a plant source and a
hothouse source of rubber; a sulfur band; the cure as a function of
composition × temperature × time; three materials and two properties
(elasticity, light absorption); four corrections that make a garment's
material matter (wet conducts, the outer layer sheds rain, wear reads
material, rubber resists acid); and **a building that says what it is
made of, storey by storey, so every place can answer the five sky
questions — and the heat question — from its construction.**

## Goals

- A player can **grow, tap or dig every input** of rubber in the realm,
  and make rubber, under-cured rubber or ebonite depending on what they
  put in the oven, how hot and for how long.
- The difference between a good cure and a bad one is **learnable by
  measuring** and describes itself in words; nothing the player knows
  or holds gates the attempt.
- **Rubber goods change what is survivable or comfortable**: dry feet
  and no shock in a flooded substation, a dry body in the rain, a pump
  seal that outlasts leather, an apron vitriol does not eat.
- **Every place answers what is over it and around it** from what it is
  built of: rain, sky light, sky view, wind and heat loss all follow the
  same construction, and a glass roof, a slate roof, a ceiling, an
  awning, a roof terrace and open sky each give the right five answers.
- **Each place says what it is made of; a structure says how its
  places stand together.** A lone hut, a single pen or an icehouse
  standing apart is complete on its own; a building, a ship or a
  multi-room pen is a structure that knows which of its places stand on
  which and which share a wall. A structure with no places (a castle on
  the horizon) is still a structure.
- **A hothouse behaves like one**: warm on a winter afternoon from the
  sun alone, freezing before dawn unless stoked, overheating at summer
  noon unless vented — and the latex tap answers to all of it.
- **Rooms in one building share heat**: a warm kitchen warms the room
  above it; an interior partition does not leak to the weather.
- *(first to cut)* **Stretch** lets one stock garment fit a range of
  bodies.

## Non-goals

- **A gate on knowing how.** No recipe refuses on the realm's epoch or on
  a held principle. → the inquiry slate's discovery loop arrives later
  as a *better way to earn the curve*, never as a lock.
- **The discovery loop** (predict, publish, replicate). → inquiry-slate.
- **Imported rubber.** No model of imports exists. → nowhere until
  logistics carries goods between regions.
- **A tropical region.** → nowhere; the stove house is how a temperate
  realm grows a tropical tree, which is historically how it was done.
- **Tyres, vehicles, plastics, oil, synthetic rubber.** →
  destructive-distillation-slate and drilling-slate; they extend this
  build's curve.
- **Sulfuring casks; vitriol making; gunpowder; matches.** →
  brewing slate / a chemistry slate; sulfur has one consumer here.
- **Glazing as pane objects; hail breaking panes.** → glass-slate.
- **Sun direction; a glass face that matters because it faces south.** →
  biome-normalization-slate.
- **A woodland canopy as a roof of leaves.** → forestry, as rows only,
  once this lands.
- **Air (smoke, CO₂) mixing between rooms of one building.** →
  structures-slate; this build moves heat between rooms, not air.
- **Footwear as a trade (a cobbler).** → textiles-slate; rubber boots
  come out of the works' mould.
- **Retiring the rest of the biome model** (dressing, air). →
  biome-normalization-slate; only sky exposure leaves the biome here.

## Placement

- **`trade-rubber`** — a new capability pack at `/trade/rubber`: the
  three materials' consumers, the works' oven and moulds, the goods, the
  latex tree's tap. A second works anywhere is rows.
- **The works and its stove house** stand in the Terminus goods yards,
  beside the oilworks, as one building with storeys (the exemplar
  multi-storey building of the content pass).
- **Russian dandelion** (*Taraxacum kok-saghyz*) is a farming crop row.
- **Hevea** (*Hevea brasiliensis*) is a forestry species row; the realm
  starts with mature stems standing in the stove house.
- **Native sulfur** is a band in the Hill Quarry's column, immediately
  above the rock salt.
- **The materials** (raw rubber, rubber, ebonite, native sulfur) are
  commons rows.
- **What is over and around a place**, and **which rooms are one
  building**, are kernel: every place in every pack answers them.

The test — *does a second instance need code?* A second hothouse, works,
rubber crop or sulfur pit is rows; a second building of any shape is a
building row.

## Collisions

- **The general store** sells leather boots (par 2); rubber boots and
  gloves join its stock rather than replacing them.
- **The tannery** makes the pump's leather packing; rubber packing is a
  competing product, not a replacement row.
- **The gas bladder** is jute and claims to be sealed; it becomes
  rubberized cloth.
- **The cafeteria atrium** is authored as open sky "because it has a
  glass roof" — it becomes a covered room with a glass roof.
- **Every Terminus interior** currently gets its sky answer from its
  biome; each keeps that answer until it says what is over it, and the
  drive's places are moved in this build.
- **The coach** keeps the cold out by its own material but not the rain;
  it answers both from its material.
- **The Drowned Substation** is world-seed content reachable only by
  teleport; it is where the boots are proved.
- **Vitriol and quicklime** are authored to corrode anything organic;
  rubber and ebonite stop being in that set.
- **The fuel contest** (charcoal, mine timber, hearth, construction,
  sugaring) gains a sixth claimant: the stove house burns every cold
  night.
- **The tailor**: stretch undercuts bespoke fit for stock garments — the
  industrial ready-to-wear story, deliberately.

## Surface decisions

### There is no knowledge gate

Vulcanization runs in the oven like any firing; the oven does not ask
who you are. What makes it industrial is that **the good window is
narrow** — too little sulfur or too short a cure weeps, too much is
ebonite, too hot scorches — so it is reached by measuring (sulfur by
weight, the oven's temperature, the time) rather than by tradition.
Watching the works teaches the process through the shipped
known-of ladder; a works that keeps its doors shut holds a head start,
never a lock. *Lens 1 chose this limb* (competence resolves detail, never
access); inquiry's codification doctrine settles the rest — the realm
has rubber because this pack shipped it.

### Two supplies of latex, two different businesses

| | Russian dandelion | Hevea under glass |
|---|---|---|
| how | a field crop; the roots are dug in autumn, milled and washed in water, and the rubber floats off | a tap on a living tree, on the shipped tap act |
| cost | land and labour, once a year | glass, a stove and fuel every cold day |
| rhythm | one harvest | a steady flow while the house is warm |
| refuses | in season only | when the house is cold — the tap says so |

Raw rubber from either is the same material: tacky when warm, stiff
when cold, good for little but rubbing out pencil.

### Sulfur is caprock

A thin native-sulfur band sits directly above the Hill Quarry's rock
salt, dug with the shipped `dig`. Honest geology (salt-dome caprock is
where sulfur forms), no new mechanism, and a player who wants it has dug
through everything else the hill had first.

### The cure is a curve with three materials on it

A charge of raw rubber and sulfur held hot in the works' oven crosslinks
at a rate set by temperature, for as long as it is held, up to what the
sulfur allows. How far it got decides what comes out:

- **under-cured** — still raw rubber, now *tacky and weeping*;
- **rubber** — graded by how near the optimum it landed; elastic, soft,
  the insulator;
- **ebonite** — from a heavy sulfur charge cured long: *hard, black,
  rings when tapped*, barely elastic, and an insulator in its own right;
- **scorched** — held too hot: worse grade, and the product says so.

The mould decides the shape: a boot, a glove, a sheet, a ring, a tube.
The curve is materials empiricism — the relationship, not the chemistry
— and the docs say so.

### Elasticity is a property of matter

Every material gains how far it stretches and still springs back: large
for rubber, near nothing for ebonite and glass, modest for leather and
knitted wool. The cure moves it (more crosslinking, less stretch); the
pump seal reads it; *(cut-first)* garment fit reads it.

### Light passes through matter by Beer–Lambert

Every material gains how strongly it absorbs light; a face of thickness
*t* passes *e^(−αt)* of what falls on it. Clear glass passes most;
anything opaque passes nothing; ice and thin horn pass some. This is the
same law the glass trade's tint already uses, and the two agree.

### What is over you and around you

⭐ **The split (decided 2026-10-16): one place, or a set.** What bounds a
place is a fact the place can decide alone, so the place says it. What
stands on what, and who shares a wall with whom, is a fact no place can
decide alone, so the structure says it.

- **A place's construction has two parts**: its **walls** and its
  **roof**, each a material and a thickness (or several layers, outermost
  first), or nothing. **The place itself says it**; a place that says
  nothing is built of the ordinary default, as every room is today.
- **A place's openings are its exits.** A doorless exit to outside is an
  open side (a three-sided shed, an open-fronted barn); a door or gate is
  a side that opens and shuts; a window passes light. No second,
  per-wall vocabulary.
- **A structure is a set of places that decide something together**: a
  building, a ship, a multi-room pen. It says which places are its
  members, and **which stands on which** — its storeys, as a small tree,
  each storey the places under an address prefix naming the storey it
  stands **on**. A tower over one wing stands on that wing. A structure
  says nothing about what anything is made of.
- **One rule decides every face**: a face is **exterior** unless the
  space across it is an **enclosed** place of the same structure — one
  that has walls or a roof of its own. So:
  - a place with an enclosed storey standing on it has a **ceiling** —
    no rain, no sky, no heat to the weather;
  - a place with nothing enclosed above it has its **roof** — and a roof
    terrace above it (a place with no walls and no roof) is open air, so
    the roof is still exterior;
  - a roof terrace belongs to the structure, and its floor **is** the
    roof below it;
  - between two members, a solid wall is a party wall, and an exit
    between them is whatever boundary it has (a gate between paddocks,
    an open stair between kitchen and loft);
  - an exit within one structure is interior; an exit out of it is
    exterior — the terraced party wall and the hall's doors both come
    out right.
- **The five answers follow from the faces**: rain reaches only through
  an open top; light through each exterior face by Beer–Lambert and
  through open exits; the sky is visible through any face that passes
  light; wind enters where there are no walls or through an open
  exterior exit; heat leaves through exterior faces and openings and
  **flows between members through interior ones**.
- **Sunlight through a transparent face warms the place** (solar gain).
- **Underground** is a place whose faces are earth.
- **Whether the floor is open ground** stops borrowing the sky answer
  and becomes a fact about the floor.
- **A place in no structure is complete**: its walls, its roof, its
  openings, every face exterior. Streets, yards and fields have no walls
  and no roof.
- **A structure with no places** — something seen from outside and never
  entered — stays valid; it has nothing for the face rule to read.
- **The default is open.** A place that says nothing has no walls and
  no roof — streets, yards and fields need no edit. **Interiors are the
  debt:** until an interior says what it is made of, it keeps the answer
  its biome gives today; **the number of interiors whose construction
  still comes from the biome is counted and may fall, never rise.** This
  build moves the drive's places; the rest follow the count, and the
  biome stops deciding the sky when it reaches zero.
- **A vessel's construction is its own material**, all round, for rain
  as well as heat — a coach is dry inside.

### The four corrections

1. **A wet material conducts.** A soaked garment's resistance falls
   toward the water's; leather boots wading salt water stop insulating.
2. **The outermost layer sheds rain.** Rain reaches the body only
   through what a garment lets through; a rubberized coat keeps you dry,
   wool does not, and the same rule makes oilskin honest.
3. **Wear reads the material.** A rubber packing outlasts a leather one
   on the same pump.
4. **Rubber and ebonite resist acid.** Vitriol eats linen and leather
   aprons and not a rubber one.

### *(first to cut)* Stretch

Within a garment's stretch — read from its material and how it is made —
being tight costs nothing and being loose is taken up. A stock garment
with an elastic waist serves a band of bodies that a plain one serves
badly. If the build is cut, this goes first, and the elasticity property
stays.

## Lens pass

1. **Pedagogy.** Chemistry gets its first *making* face: the
   sulfur × temperature × time curve, the over-range material, scorch.
   Physics: the greenhouse effect (light in through glass, heat held,
   lost at night), Beer–Lambert, conduction through wet matter, heat
   rising through a ceiling. Earth science: caprock over salt. Botany:
   latex is in many plants, not one tree. ⚠ The honest limit, stated:
   this teaches the **curve**, not polymer chemistry.
2. **Expression.** A new material re-makes every garment, tool and vessel
   row in rubber with no code. A roof of glass, slate, thatch, leaves or
   ice is a row; a building of any shape — storeys, a tower over a wing,
   a roof terrace, an awning, an atrium — is a building row. **Constant:**
   the cure curve, Beer–Lambert, the face rule, conduction. **Variable:**
   what each building is made of and how it stacks, what the works makes,
   what the hothouse grows. Named works: a Kew palm house — passes; a
   Victorian terrace with a roof garden — passes; *Fitzcarraldo* — fails
   (no tropics, by design, recorded).
3. **Immersion and participation.** The stove house has jobs nobody
   scripts: stoke it before dawn, vent it at noon. The boots are proved
   by wading, not by a number. The economy gains rubber-shaped holes a
   player fills: the dandelion grower, the tapper, the caprock digger,
   the works hand, the stoker.
4. **Values.** No gate means the works' secret is the curve in its
   people's heads — a head start, never a lock; cold discovery stays
   open by physics. Rubber undercuts the tanner (packing) and the tailor
   (stretch); that is the lesson of the industrial epoch, not a balance
   bug, and nothing protects them from it.
5. **Continuity.** Rubber boots, packing and coats answer the same
   commands as the leather and wool they replace. The cure is the polymer
   curve that plastics will extend. The building model is the same for a
   medieval hall, a Victorian hothouse and a ship's decks.
6. **Economy.** Produces insulation, waterproofing, seals, acid gear,
   stretch. Consumes land, glass, fuel (now contested six ways), sulfur,
   labour. Who pays: the substation's workers, anyone out in the rain,
   the pump's owner, whoever handles vitriol. **The demand was there
   first**: the substation named rubber boots; rain, the pump and vitriol
   all shipped before rubber did.
7. **Governance.** Nothing judges a person. Every refusal is physical —
   the house is cold, there is no sulfur, the oven is not hot enough —
   and says so.

## The drive

The drive runs against a freshly dropped world. Each step names what the
player must see; "not refused" is never success.

**The sky and the building**

1. Stand in the **cafeteria atrium** in rain: you stay dry, `look` sees
   the sky, `measure light` reads daylight through glass, and it is
   warmer than the street.
2. Stand under a **market awning** in rain: dry, and `feel` reports the
   wind.
3. Board a **coach** standing in rain: dry inside.
4. In the **works**, light the stove in the ground-floor room: within the
   hour the loft above reads warmer than it did, and warmer than an
   unheated loft would.
5. Climb to the works' **roof terrace** in rain: wet, open sky, the floor
   reads as the roof's material; the room below it stays dry.
6. Stand in the room under the works' **tower**, then in a ground-floor
   room not under it: in a storm the first is covered, the second is
   under the roof.

**The stove house**

7. A clear winter afternoon, stove out: `measure temperature` reads warm
   — the sun did it.
8. Before dawn, stove out: cold; `tap` on a Hevea stem refuses, saying
   it is too cold.
9. Light the stove and wait: the house warms; the tap opens.
10. A clear summer noon, vents shut: the house overheats past the tree's
    comfort; open the door/vents and it falls.

**Supply**

11. Tap latex into a cup; it sets into **raw rubber**, which reads tacky
    when warm and stiff when cold.
12. Dig Russian dandelion roots in autumn, mill and wash them, and get
    raw rubber the other way.
13. Dig down the Hill Quarry through the caprock: a lump of **native
    sulfur**.

**The cure**

14. In the works' oven, a short low-sulfur cure: *tacky, weeping* —
    under-cured.
15. A measured charge at the right temperature and time: **rubber**, of
    good grade, in the boot mould.
16. A heavy-sulfur charge cured long: **ebonite** — *hard, black, rings
    when tapped*.
17. The same good charge held too hot: scorched, and it says so.

**The goods**

18. Wade the **Drowned Substation** in leather boots: they soak through
    and you are shocked. In rubber boots: unharmed.
19. Walk in rain in a **rubberized coat**: your body stays dry. In a wool
    coat: wet.
20. Pack the pump with **rubber**, stroke it as many times as wore the
    leather packing to *worn*: the rubber reads *sound*.
21. Spill vitriol on a **linen apron** and a **rubber apron**: the linen
    is eaten, the rubber is not.
22. The **general store**'s stock lists rubber boots and gloves, and they
    can be bought.
23. *(cut-first)* A heavy body tries a plain stock garment (tight) and an
    elastic-waisted one: the elastic one is not tight.

## Acceptance criteria

1. Every rubber input — latex, dandelion rubber, sulfur — is obtainable
   by a player in the realm, with nothing granted.
2. The cure gives under-cured rubber, rubber, ebonite or scorched rubber
   according to sulfur amount, temperature and time, and each describes
   itself in words a player can tell apart.
3. Nothing about who the player is or what they know prevents attempting
   the cure.
4. The stove house's temperature rises with sun through its glass and
   with its stove, falls at night, and the latex tap opens and closes
   with it, saying why it is closed.
5. In rain: the atrium, an awning, a coach interior and any covered room
   are dry; a roof terrace and the street are wet.
6. A room's light, sky view, wind and heat loss agree with what is over
   and around it: a glass roof lets in light and sky, a ceiling lets in
   neither, an awning lets in wind.
7. Heat from one room reaches the room above it in the same building;
   an interior wall loses nothing to the weather.
8. Leather boots soaked in the substation shock you; rubber boots do not.
9. A rubberized coat keeps the body dry in rain; a wool coat does not.
10. A rubber packing outlasts a leather one on the same pump.
11. Vitriol damages a linen apron and not a rubber one.
12. Rubber boots and gloves can be bought at the general store.
13. Every place the drive visits answers the sky and heat questions
    from what it says it is made of and the structure it is in (if any);
    the number of interiors whose construction still comes from the biome
    is counted and does not rise.
15. A place in no structure (a lone hut, a pen) and a structure with no
    places (a landmark seen from outside) both work: the first answers
    every question on its own, the second still stands on the horizon.
14. *(cut-first)* An elastic garment fits a body a plain stock garment of
    the same cut is tight on.
