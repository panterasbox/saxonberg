# Paint {#paint}

Paint is a binder with color ground into it. The binder is what sets and
holds — linseed oil, lime, size, an egg — and the pigment is what you see:
an earth dug and ground, chalk, soot, lead white. There is no list of
paints. Any binder that sets takes any pigment, and the color is what the
pigments are, mixed by how much of each went in.

## Making it {#making-it}

The colorman's stones in Wharfside do the grinding. `mill flax` presses
oil into a jar; `mill clay` grinds an ochre earth to a paper of pigment.
Pour the oil into a pot, put the pigment in, and `stir pot` — a few
minutes' stirring (a paint is runny; `knead` is for a stiff mass like
clay or dough): the pot now holds a paint, and `look` at it says what it is made of and what color it is.
Crack an egg into it and it binds as tempera instead. Some pigments
hide what is under them better than others, and some fade in the sun.

## Painting {#painting}

With a brush in hand, `paint <thing> with <pot>` lays one coat. The thing
painted decides how much it takes: a board a little, a house front a great
deal. A building is painted from outside, where it meets the weather —
the lane in front of it, the yard, a quay a ship lies against. From there
`look` at a building to see its coat, `analyze condition` it for the state
of the fabric under it and why, and `measure paint` it (a rule or a gauging
rod) for roughly how much paint one coat would take.

A coat goes on wet. Lime wants air and time; oil wants air and warms a
little as it sets. Rain on a wet coat makes it run, and frost crazes it,
so pick your day.

`write <words> on <board> with <pot>` letters a sign in paint. Anyone can
read it — until the weather takes the coat, and the words with it.

## Weather {#weather}

The weather wears a coat away wherever it reaches it: an oil lasts years,
a limewash a season or two. Two coats last twice as long as one, and a coat
that ran or crazed goes faster. Indoors, nothing wears. Once the coat on a
building is gone, the weather gets into the fabric under it — sills,
putty, boards — and a householder's kit and a morning's work (`maintain`)
put that right. The kit mends the fabric; only a brush puts the coat back.

## Glue {#glue}

A glue pot boils hide scraps, bone or fish skins into glue; curd and lime
make casein, flour makes paste. A glued joint takes glue from a pot in
reach when it is fitted, and is weak until the glue has set. Hide glue
lets go in the damp and comes apart again with heat and steam; casein
holds through the wet.

## Hazards {#hazards}

Lead white is poison: grinding it and brushing it put lead in you, and
gloves help. Rags soaked in oil heat themselves in a heap and can catch —
spread them out. Turpentine fills a room with fumes; open air clears them.

See `help paint`, `help measure`, `help maintain` for the commands.
