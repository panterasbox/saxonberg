# The Paramount Decree {#the-paramount-decree}

The law that broke the integrated houses — and the reason a dead
corporate sign still hangs over an independent blood window.

## The integrated trade

Once, a house like Goodkin held all three links of the blood trade at
once: it **collected** the blood, it **carried** it cold across the
city, and it **gave it out** at the window where the sick came. Holding
all three is the crime the Decree named — not any single link, but the
grip of one hand on the whole chain, so that the price of being saved
was whatever the house that saved you cared to charge.

## Two of three

The Decree let a house keep **two** links and made it give up the
**third**. Goodkin — like the others — chose which to shed by the ledger,
and the giving-away was the least profitable link: a window that takes no
money from a donor and little enough from a patient is a cost centre, not
a mine. So Goodkin kept **collection** and **carting** (where the money
was) and divested the **window**.

## The name that stayed

The windows went independent — bought, or simply let go, and run by
whoever already stood behind the bars. But the signs stayed. For years
you could walk the city and read the old house names in dead neon over
doors those houses no longer kept: GOODKIN BLOODWORKS over a window
Goodkin had not run since the Decree, its tubes grey, nobody paying to
relight them.

The registrar behind the bars will tell you, if you ask and she is in
the mood: *"We keep the name. The sign's theirs; the blood's yours."*
The carts outside are still Goodkin's. The window is not.
