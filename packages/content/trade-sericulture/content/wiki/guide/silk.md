# Silk {#silk}

Silk is the thread a silkworm spins round itself to become a moth. To
have the thread you have to stop the moth: everything you take for silk
ends the animal, and everything you keep for next year is silk you did
not reel.

## Rearing {#rearing}

A tray of worms starts from an egg lot set on a warm tray
(`put egg lot in tray`). The worms age by the warmth of the room, not by
the calendar, so a cold room is a slow tray. They eat cut mulberry leaf
put on the tray; a hungry tray does not die, it grows slowly and spins
light cocoons. Give the wandering worms a brush to climb and they spin on
time. The tray tells you in words how it is doing, never in numbers.

Some lots carry a disease no one can see. A sick tray eats poorly and
shows dark specks late in its life; a badly sick one dies before it
spins.

## The deadline {#the-deadline}

Cocoons come off the tray sorted heavy, middling and light. They must be
**stifled** (held hot long enough to kill the pupa inside) within days,
or the moths break out and the thread is cut. Too hot for too long
scorches them. A stifling oven is gentle; a kitchen range works if you
watch it.

What you do not stifle hatches and lays eggs where it stands. Keep the
heavy cocoons for eggs and the next generation weighs more; keep the
light and it weighs less. Feeding a tray badly makes light cocoons but not
light worms: only what is inherited carries on.

## Reeling {#reeling}

Stifled cocoons are unwound in hot water onto a reel, a few cocoons to
the thread. Fewer cocoons make a finer thread, slower and harder to keep
even; how even it comes out is the reeler's skill and the reel's.

The skein is raw silk, still gummed. It is spun and woven like any other
fiber; the gum boils off in the cloth.
