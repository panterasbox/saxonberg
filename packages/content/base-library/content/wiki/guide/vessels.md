# What a vessel holds {#vessels}

A jug, a crock, a cask and a boat all answer the same two questions: does
it keep what is in it, and what does it do to it? Neither answer is written
on the vessel. Both come out of what it is made of, how it was made, and
what has happened to it since.

## Does it hold? {#holding}

A vessel loses liquid three ways, and they add up.

- **Through its body.** Unglazed earthenware is full of tiny pores, and
  water creeps through them. A water jar of plain terracotta weeps: its
  outside is always damp, and the damp drying off keeps the water inside
  cool. A glaze that holds water seals the pores, and so does pitch.
- **Through a crack or a hole.** A crack lets liquid out whatever the
  vessel is made of, glaze or no glaze, and a hole lets out a great deal
  more. The deeper the liquid, the faster it goes.
- **Through its joints.** A cask is staves held by hoops. A sprung stave,
  or hoops gone slack, let the cask run out: a full cask with one sprung
  stave is visibly lower within the hour. A cask left empty dries, its
  hoops go slack, and a refill runs straight through it.

What runs out has to go somewhere. It goes into whatever the vessel stands
in or on: a jar on a dish fills the dish, a sugar cone on its pot drains
into the pot. Failing that, it goes onto the floor and makes a puddle. A
jug leaking inside a pack soaks the pack first, then drips onto the floor
at your feet. Nothing simply disappears.

A vessel that cannot hold a liquid lets a pour run straight through it.
`look` at a vessel to see what has failed, and `look` again later to see
how far the level has fallen.

## Mending it {#mending}

- A sprung stave is replaced: `fit stave to cask`, with a cooper's driver
  and a hand that knows the trade. Staves are fitted one at a time. Slack
  hoops are driven back down with `repair`.
- A hole stove in a boat or a ship is patched with `repair`, using stock of
  whatever her hull is made of, and the tool that works it. A ship's
  caulking is kept up with `maintain`.
- A crack in fired clay can be riveted by someone who knows how.

See `help fit`, `help repair` and `help pour`.

## What the wall gives {#the-wall}

Whatever a liquid sits against, it takes something from.

- **The wall's own material.** Oak gives what it holds the taste and smell
  of oak, and a little vanilla. A cask burnt black inside gives vanilla and
  char. That is most of what makes whiskey taste of the cask. Even water
  in an oak pail tastes faintly of oak. A wall gives most to its first fill
  and less to each fill after, until it has nothing left. `look` at a cask
  to see how much it still has to give. A spent cask can be re-fired with
  `repair`, which burns a fresh char inside it.
- **What the wall remembers.** A wall that is not sealed (terracotta, oak)
  soaks up some of what it held, smell and all, and gives it back to the
  next thing you put in it. Water in an old whiskey cask smells of whiskey.
  A crock that held cream gives a little of it to the next milk. What it
  gives back, it loses. `wash` a vessel to clear it faster: each wash takes
  away up to half of what the wall has kept.
- **A sealed wall gives nothing of its own.** A glazed crock remembers
  nothing, and a pitched ale cask keeps the oak out of the ale. A lead glaze
  is the exception: it gives lead to what it holds, more to anything sour.
  See [[Giving to what it holds]].

## Whose is it? {#whose}

What is in a vessel has its own quality and its own maker, and so does the
vessel. A fine wine in a badly made bottle is still a fine wine, and
filling a potter's jug does not make it yours. `look` shows how good the
contents are. `analyze condition` reads the vessel itself, including who
made it. Topping something up with a worse batch makes all of it worse.

A vessel that gives its shape to what sets in it, like a mould or a cone,
also passes on its own quality: a poor mould makes a poor casting, however
fine the metal poured into it.

## Out in the rain {#rain}

Rain wets what it falls on, and how much depends on where a thing stands.
A thing under a roof takes none. A glazed pot sheds most of the rain off its
surface and takes none of it into its body. A terracotta pot beside it gets
wet on the outside and soaks the rain in, up to what its body can hold.
Green bricks left out in the weather take up water the same way, which is
why a brickmaker dries them under cover.
