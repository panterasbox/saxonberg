# Tracts and fields {#tracts-and-fields}

A farm's ground comes in two sizes. A **tract** is open ground you walk
onto — the bench above a farmyard, the meadow below it, a cottage garden.
A **field** is what you plant: a part of a tract you have staked out and
named, with soil, grass and a history of its own.

Nobody makes a tract. A farm is a holding with a layout — a yard, a barn,
a farmhouse and its tracts — and every farm sold from the same layout has
the same tracts, sized to the ground it was sold with. A farm on the far
side of the lane is the same farm turned round, so its yard still opens
on the lane.

## Marking a field {#marking-a-field}

Stand on a tract you hold and pace one out: `mark barley field as a third
of it`. A share is said the way a farmer says it — a third, half,
two-thirds, a quarter, all of it, or a per cent; say none and it takes the
rest. Mark the same name again to move its line; `unmark` takes the stakes
up, and the ground goes back to the rest of the tract with whatever you did
to its soil. You cannot move a line through a standing crop, and you
cannot mark more than there is.

Ground nobody has marked is rough. Nothing is sown on it, but you can still
read its soil from where you stand. From a yard you can read a tract's
fields over the gate: `look upper bench`.

## Working a field {#working-a-field}

On a tract of several fields, every act on a crop or its ground names the
field: `plough at the wheat field`, `sow barley at the barley field`,
`mow at the far side`. A herd grazes a field, not a place — `move the herd
to the far side` — and a field with a crop on it is never grazed. A trough
on the tract feeds any field's herd.

## What the ground allows {#what-the-ground-allows}

Zoning decides whether ground takes fields at all. A house lot is zoned
for living, and its garden takes beds, not fields — try to mark one and
you are told why. Farmland takes fields. See `help mark`.
